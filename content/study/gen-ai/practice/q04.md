## What's actually being asked
Define joint vs. conditional probability precisely, show the difference with an example, and give two applications of models built on each.

## Answer

### Joint probability
`P(x, y)` is the probability that **both** events `x` and `y` happen together, with no event treated as "given." It describes the full picture of how two (or more) variables co-occur.

**Example:** let `x` = "patient has fever" and `y` = "patient has flu". `P(x, y)` is the probability that a randomly picked patient has *both* fever and flu simultaneously.

### Conditional probability
`P(y|x)` is the probability of `y` **given that `x` is already known/observed**. It's a probability over a restricted world — only the subset of cases where `x` is true.

**Same example:** `P(y|x)` is the probability that a patient has flu, *restricted to* the patients who already have fever. This is usually a larger number than `P(x,y)` alone, because conditioning narrows the sample space rather than asking about two things jointly.

### The fundamental difference
Joint probability asks "how likely are both together, out of everyone," while conditional probability asks "given I already know one happened, how likely is the other." They're related by the basic identity:
`P(x, y) = P(y|x) · P(x) = P(x|y) · P(y)`
— this identity is exactly what lets you convert one into the other, and it's the seed of Bayes' theorem (see Q7).

### Two applications of joint-probability-based (generative) models
1. **Naïve Bayes classification** — models `P(x,y) = P(y)·P(x|y)` (feature likelihoods times class prior) to classify documents/emails, then uses Bayes' theorem to get `P(y|x)`.
2. **Bayesian networks** — model a joint distribution over many correlated variables (e.g., symptoms and diseases in a medical diagnosis network) to reason about any variable given any subset of others.

### Two applications of conditional-probability-based (discriminative) models
1. **Logistic regression / SVM classification** — directly models `P(y|x)` (or a decision function) without ever modeling how `x` itself is distributed; used for spam detection, credit risk scoring.
2. **Autoregressive language modeling** — a transformer-based LLM predicts `P(next word | previous words)` directly at each generation step, never explicitly modeling the joint distribution of entire sentences.

### Why the distinction matters beyond definitions
This is the exact same split that separates generative and discriminative learning paradigms (see Q5–Q7): a model built on the joint `P(x,y)` can answer *any* probabilistic question about the data — including generating new `x` — because the joint contains everything. A model built only on `P(y|x)` answers one narrow question very well (what's the label?) but has no notion of how likely a given `x` even is on its own, so it can't generate anything or detect "this input looks nothing like anything I've seen."
