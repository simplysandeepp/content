## What's actually being asked
Give the full picture: the architecture of both networks with conditioning wired in, the step-by-step training loop, the formal objective, and a balanced discussion of what controlled generation buys you and where it falls short.

## Answer

### Architecture

**Generator `G(z|y)`**
- Inputs: noise `z ∼ p_z` (e.g. `N(0, I)`) and condition `y` (class label, embedding, or source image).
- `y` is turned into a usable vector (one-hot or learned embedding) and injected by concatenating it with `z` at the input (simplest case), or via conditional batch-norm / feature-map concatenation at intermediate layers in deeper architectures.
- Hidden layers: Linear (vanilla cGAN) or transposed-conv (conv/DCGAN-style cGAN), ReLU activations, progressively building up to the data shape.
- Output layer: Tanh (or Sigmoid, depending on data scaling) → `G(z|y)`, a sample intended to be both realistic and consistent with `y`.

**Discriminator `D(x|y)`**
- Inputs: a sample `x` (real or `G(z|y)`) and the same condition `y`.
- `y` injected the same way as in G — concatenation at the input or at a feature map, or via a projection-discriminator inner-product term.
- Hidden layers: Linear or conv stack, LeakyReLU activations.
- Output layer: a single Sigmoid neuron → `D(x|y) ∈ (0,1)`, the probability that `x` is a real sample *consistent with `y`*.

### Diagram
See the reference diagram: `(z, y)` flows into G to produce a fake sample; both the fake sample (with its `y`) and real samples (with their true `y`) flow into the same D instance, producing the adversarial score used for both losses.

### The conditional adversarial objective
`min_G max_D 𝔼[log D(x|y)] + 𝔼[log(1 − D(G(z|y)|y))]`

`y` appears in every term: paired with real `x` in the first expectation, and paired with the noise fed to G (then reused when scoring the resulting fake) in the second.

### Training process, step by step
1. Sample a batch of real pairs `(x, y) ∼ p_data`.
2. Sample a batch of noise `z ∼ p_z` together with labels `y` (matching the same marginal distribution — often simply reusing the real batch's labels).
3. Forward `(z, y)` through G → fake batch `G(z|y)`.
4. **Discriminator step**: forward the real pairs `(x,y)` and the fake pairs `(G(z|y), y)` through D; compute D's loss `−[log D(x|y) + log(1 − D(G(z|y)|y))]`; backpropagate and update **D's weights only** (G frozen).
5. **Generator step**: forward a fresh `(z, y)` through G, then through the just-updated D; compute G's loss (non-saturating form, standard in practice) `−log D(G(z|y)|y)`; backpropagate through D into G, but update **G's weights only** (D frozen).
6. Repeat for many iterations/epochs, alternating D and G steps, until `D(x|y)` and `D(G(z|y)|y)` both hover near 0.5 (the ideal equilibrium), or in practice until sample quality and conditional fidelity plateau.

This is structurally identical to vanilla GAN training — the only change is that `y` rides along with every sample, on both the real and fake side, through both networks' forward passes.

### Advantages of controlled generation
- **Controllability** — choose `y` at inference time to steer exactly what gets generated (a class, an attribute, a source image to translate), instead of getting an arbitrary sample from the marginal distribution.
- **Sharper conditional modes** — D's joint realism-and-consistency check (rejecting correct-looking-but-wrong-label samples) pushes G to commit clearly to each condition rather than hedging or blurring across classes.
- **One reusable model** — a single trained G covers all conditions, rather than needing a separate unconditional GAN per class or category.
- **Enables practically useful pipelines** — text-to-image, image-to-image translation, class-balanced data augmentation — none of which a plain unconditional GAN can do on demand.

### Limitations
- **No extra training stability** — a cGAN is still fully susceptible to mode collapse, vanishing gradients, and training instability; conditioning adds control, not a fix for the underlying adversarial-training pathologies, so the same remedies covered for vanilla GANs still apply, layered on top.
- **Label/condition quality bottleneck** — conditional fidelity is only as good as the `(x,y)` pairing in training data; noisy or imbalanced labels propagate directly into inconsistent or biased conditional generation.
- **Harder discriminator task** — D must learn a *joint* realism-and-consistency boundary over `(x,y)`, a strictly harder problem than plain realism, which can need more data or capacity, especially as the space of `y` grows (many fine-grained classes, or free-form text).
- **Mode collapse within a condition** — G can still collapse to a handful of outputs *per `y`* even while nominally respecting the condition; conditioning narrows *what* collapses, not *whether* it can happen.
