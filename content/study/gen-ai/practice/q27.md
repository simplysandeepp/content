## What's actually being asked
Lay the three objectives side by side and analyze the gradient signal each one gives, at different stages of training, and what that implies for how stable training is.

## Answer

### The three objectives
1. **Discriminator loss** (same regardless of which G variant is used): D maximizes `log D(x) + log(1 − D(G(z)))`.
2. **Saturating Generator loss** (original minimax): G minimizes `log(1 − D(G(z)))`.
3. **Non-saturating Generator loss** (practical default): G maximizes `log D(G(z))`.

### Gradient analysis
Let `d = D(G(z))` and look at the slope of each G objective with respect to `d`.

- **Saturating**: loss `= log(1 − d)`, derivative `= −1/(1 − d)`. Early in training `d ≈ 0` (D easily spots fakes). The curve `log(1−d)` is flat near `d=0` and only gets steep as `d → 1`. So the gradient that actually reaches G's parameters (through the chain rule) is informative *late* — once G is already winning — and nearly vanishing exactly when G most needs guidance. This is the textbook vanishing-gradient failure for G.
- **Non-saturating**: loss `= −log d`, derivative `= −1/d`. Near `d ≈ 0` (G currently bad) this magnitude is large — a strong gradient exactly when G needs it. As `d → 1` the gradient naturally shrinks — steep when needed, gentle near convergence. A much better-behaved training signal.

### Discriminator's loss and stability
D's own loss doesn't change between the two G variants — it's always plain binary cross-entropy. D's gradient *quality* depends instead on the capacity balance between D and G: an over-powerful D confidently saturates (`D(x)→1`, `D(G(z))→0`) regardless of which G loss is in use, starving G of useful gradient under the saturating loss, and still demanding careful balancing even under the non-saturating loss.

### Stability comparison

| Objective | Early gradient for G | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Saturating G loss | weak / vanishing | G stalls, D "wins" trivially, training gets stuck |
| Non-saturating G loss | strong | avoids the stall, but is no longer a clean zero-sum minimax game — no strict theoretical convergence guarantee, can still oscillate |
| D loss (either case) | depends on D/G capacity balance | D too strong starves both G variants; D too weak gives noisy, uninformative gradient to G |

### Bottom line
Non-saturating loss is the practical default precisely because it fixes the saturating objective's early-training vanishing-gradient problem, at the cost of giving up the clean game-theoretic symmetry of the original minimax formulation — it becomes a heuristic, non-zero-sum game. That's part of why GAN training, while far more usable with the non-saturating loss, still has no guaranteed convergence and needs care (learning-rate balance between G and D, label smoothing, etc.) on top of the loss choice.

## Exam tip
When asked to "compare," show the derivative/slope argument explicitly (why `log(1−d)` is flat near `d=0` but `−log d` is steep there) — that concrete mechanism is what separates a full-marks answer from "non-saturating is better because it's used in practice."
