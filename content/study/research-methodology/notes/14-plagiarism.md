## The idea, in plain words

**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words, ideas, data, images, or even your own previously
published work as if they were new — without giving proper credit. It isn't only "copy-pasting a
paragraph" — rewording someone's argument without citing them still counts.

## The four types

- **Direct / verbatim plagiarism** — copying text word-for-word from a source with no quotation
  marks and no citation. The most obvious and most heavily penalised type.
- **Paraphrasing / mosaic plagiarism** — rewriting someone's sentence or idea in your own words but
  still presenting it as your own, without citing where the idea came from. Called "mosaic" because
  it's often built by stitching together reworded fragments from several sources.
- **Self-plagiarism** — reusing substantial parts of your *own* earlier published work (a past
  assignment, thesis chapter, or paper) in a new piece without disclosing that it's been published
  before. Still dishonest, because it misrepresents the new work as entirely original.
- **Accidental plagiarism** — unintentional: forgetting a citation, paraphrasing too close to the
  original wording, or not realising a fact needed a source. Unintentional doesn't mean
  consequence-free — institutions usually still penalise it, just typically less severely than
  deliberate plagiarism.

Many institutions (including UGC-regulated ones in India) formalise this with a **similarity-index
tier system** from plagiarism-detection software: roughly, a low similarity score (under ~10%) is
treated as incidental and ignored, while progressively higher bands trigger escalating penalties —
resubmission, a one-year bar on submitting work, up to cancellation of registration for the most
severe, repeated cases. The exact thresholds vary by institution, but the pattern — "a little
overlap is normal, a lot is a serious offence" — is the point to remember.

## Six ways to avoid it

1. **Cite every source** you draw from — both direct quotes and ideas you've paraphrased.
2. **Use quotation marks** for any text copied word-for-word, however short.
3. **Paraphrase properly** — don't just swap a few synonyms; genuinely rewrite the idea in your own
   structure and words, and still cite the original source.
4. **Run a plagiarism-detection check** (e.g. Turnitin) on your own draft before submitting it, so
   you catch accidental overlaps yourself.
5. **Keep a working bibliography** as you research, so you never lose track of where an idea came
   from and end up guessing or skipping a citation later.
6. **Get permission / give credit** for data, images, or unpublished material that isn't yours —
   including disclosing when you're reusing your own prior work.

## Consequences of plagiarism

- **Academic:** failing the assignment, course, or having a degree revoked if discovered after the
  fact; formal disciplinary proceedings.
- **Professional:** retraction of published papers, loss of research credibility, damage to a
  researcher's reputation that can follow them for years.
- **Legal:** copyright infringement claims if the copied material was protected — this is where
  plagiarism and IPR (intellectual property rights) directly overlap, since using someone's
  copyrighted text, images, or data without permission can be a legal violation, not just an
  academic one.
- **Institutional:** the university or publisher's own credibility and accreditation can be
  affected by a pattern of unpunished plagiarism.
- **To the original author:** they lose the credit and citation count that is often the entire
  "currency" of academic work — plagiarism is a real harm to a specific person, not a victimless
  rule-break.

**Example:** a student copies three paragraphs from a journal article into their report with no
citation — caught by a similarity check, the report is failed and the student faces a formal
hearing. A researcher reuses data from their own earlier published study without disclosing it in a
new paper — the paper is later retracted when a reviewer notices the overlap, and the researcher's
other work comes under scrutiny too.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Real mid-term, Q6 (K3, 5 marks): "Evaluate the potential consequences of plagiarism in a research paper. Support your answer with relevant examples."

**Answer plan:** cover the five consequence categories above (academic, professional, legal,
institutional, harm to the original author) — at K3/5 marks, "Evaluate... support with examples"
wants more than a list, so attach one concrete example to at least two or three of the categories
(use the two examples above, or adapt them). Close with one line tying consequences back to *why*
plagiarism is taken so seriously: it breaks the basic trust that research results are genuinely the
author's own verified work.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q7: "Evaluate the six ways to avoid plagiarism and propose measures an institution could adopt to prevent it."

**Answer plan:** list the six prevention methods above, then add an institutional layer on top —
mandatory plagiarism-detection checks before submission, plagiarism-awareness training for new
students/researchers, clear written policy with defined similarity-index thresholds and penalties,
and a named academic-integrity officer or committee to review flagged cases fairly.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q9: "Critique the four types of plagiarism based on their severity and formulate a strategy to detect them."

**Answer plan:** rank the four types by severity — deliberate direct/verbatim plagiarism is
typically treated most severely (clear intent, easiest to prove), self-plagiarism and mosaic
plagiarism next (intent is often present but harder to prove conclusively), accidental plagiarism
least severely (no intent, but still a lapse in care). For detection: similarity-index software
catches direct and much mosaic plagiarism; self-plagiarism needs cross-referencing against an
author's own publication history (software alone often misses this); accidental plagiarism is best
caught by careful self-review against a citation checklist before submission.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q10: "Construct a scenario illustrating accidental plagiarism and propose suitable corrective measures."

**Answer plan — example scenario:** a student paraphrases a textbook definition closely while taking
notes, then later drops that paraphrase into their report, having forgotten it wasn't originally
their own wording and forgetting to attach a citation. **Corrective measures:** always cite at the
moment of writing a paraphrase (not "add citations later"), keep a reference manager (Zotero,
Mendeley) running throughout the research process so every note is pre-linked to its source, and run
a similarity check on the finished draft as a final safety net before submission.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q12: "Formulate a comprehensive plagiarism and research ethics policy suitable for a university or research institute."

**Answer plan:** structure it as named sections — (1) **Definition** of plagiarism and its types (as
above); (2) **Prevention**, the six methods above, made mandatory; (3) **Detection**, required
similarity-index checks with a specified threshold before any submission is accepted; (4)
**Tiered penalties**, scaled to severity (minor similarity → resubmission; moderate → formal
warning/grade penalty; severe or repeated → suspension or expulsion); (5) **Appeals process**, so
flagged cases get a fair review rather than an automatic penalty; (6) **Training**, mandatory
academic-integrity orientation for all new students/researchers. This mirrors the broader research
ethics framework the rest of this unit covers (see "15. Research Ethics").
