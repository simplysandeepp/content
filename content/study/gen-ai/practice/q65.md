## What's actually being asked

Compare four distinct prompting strategies — instruction, role, one/few-shot, and structured reasoning (chain-of-thought) prompting — on purpose, pros, cons, and where each fits in real GenAI applications.

## Answer

### 1. Instruction prompting

**Purpose**: state the task directly in natural language — "Summarize this article in 3 bullet points," "Translate this to French." Relies on the model having been instruction-tuned (RLHF/SFT) to follow commands rather than just continue text statistically.

- **Advantages**: simplest to write, no examples needed, flexible across almost any task, cheapest in tokens.
- **Limitations**: ambiguous or underspecified instructions give inconsistent output ("summarize" — how long? what tone?); quality depends heavily on how well the underlying model was instruction-tuned.
- **Suitable applications**: general-purpose chatbots, content drafting, direct Q&A, everyday assistant tasks.

### 2. Role prompting

**Purpose**: assign the model a persona or expertise framing before the task — "You are a senior data scientist explaining this to a junior analyst." Steers tone, vocabulary, and the *framing* of the answer.

- **Advantages**: produces more domain-appropriate style and consistency across a conversation; useful for simulated personas, tutoring tones, or brand voice.
- **Limitations**: a role doesn't grant actual capability — "You are an expert cardiologist" doesn't make the model medically more correct, only more confident-sounding, which can be actively misleading (overconfidence risk) if mistaken for real expertise.
- **Suitable applications**: tutoring bots, customer-support agents, creative-writing personas, style-consistent brand assistants.

### 3. One-shot / few-shot prompting

**Purpose**: show the model one or more input→output examples in the prompt itself, teaching the desired *pattern* (format, label vocabulary, reasoning style) via in-context learning, with no weight updates.

- **Advantages**: works with a completely frozen model, fast to iterate (just edit the prompt), effective for niche formats or label schemes the model wasn't explicitly tuned for.
- **Limitations**: consumes context-window tokens (cost/latency grows with example count); sensitive to the order and choice of examples; doesn't scale well to genuinely complex, multi-step reasoning on its own.
- **Suitable applications**: structured data extraction into a fixed schema, classification with a custom label set, style transfer/imitation tasks.

### 4. Structured reasoning prompting (chain-of-thought)

**Purpose**: explicitly ask the model to produce intermediate reasoning steps before the final answer — "Let's think step by step," or a template that forces a reasoning → conclusion structure. Elicits the model's latent step-by-step computation rather than forcing it to jump straight to an answer.

- **Advantages**: measurably better accuracy on arithmetic, logic, and multi-hop questions versus asking for the answer directly; makes the reasoning auditable — a human can check *where* it went wrong, not just that it's wrong.
- **Limitations**: longer outputs mean higher latency/cost; the written reasoning can look plausible while not actually reflecting what produced the answer (unfaithful chains) — so it's not a guarantee of correctness, just a strong accuracy aid.
- **Suitable applications**: math/logic solvers, code-debugging assistants, multi-step planning agents, anywhere a wrong one-shot answer is costly enough to want an inspectable trail.

### Summary table

| Strategy | Purpose | Key strength | Key weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instruction | Direct task command | Simple, flexible | Ambiguity → inconsistency |
| Role | Persona/style framing | Consistent tone/domain fit | No real capability boost |
| One/few-shot | Teach pattern via examples | Works on frozen model | Token cost, order-sensitive |
| Structured reasoning | Elicit step-by-step reasoning | Better multi-step accuracy | Longer, not always faithful |

In practice these combine — e.g., "You are a math tutor (role). Solve step by step (reasoning). Here's an example (few-shot): ..." is a perfectly normal real-world prompt stacking all four.

## Exam tip

If asked to pick *one* for a "multi-step word problem" style question, the expected answer is structured reasoning (chain-of-thought) — that's the strategy specifically shown (qualitatively) to help with multi-hop/arithmetic tasks, not instruction or role prompting alone.
