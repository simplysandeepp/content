## What's actually being asked
Work out `α_t`, the cumulative products `ᾱ_t`, and the signal-to-noise term `√ᾱ_t / √(1−ᾱ_t)` for a 3-step DDPM schedule, then explain the trend across steps.

## Answer

**Note on the numbers:** the source text for this question lost the actual `β_1, β_2, β_3` values during copy-paste — they never made it into what's given here. So below is the method worked fully and correctly on an **illustrative** 3-step schedule (`β_1 = 0.1, β_2 = 0.2, β_3 = 0.3`) — swap in the real numbers and redo these same three steps if you get them.

### Step 1 — α_t
`α_t = 1 − β_t`

| t | β_t | α_t = 1 − β_t |
|---|-----|---------------|
| 1 | 0.1 | 0.9 |
| 2 | 0.2 | 0.8 |
| 3 | 0.3 | 0.7 |

### Step 2 — cumulative product ᾱ_t
`ᾱ_t = Π_{s=1}^{t} α_s` — multiply every α up to and including step t.

- `ᾱ_1 = α_1 = 0.9`
- `ᾱ_2 = α_1·α_2 = 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72`
- `ᾱ_3 = α_1·α_2·α_3 = 0.72 × 0.7 = 0.504`

`ᾱ_t` only ever shrinks as t grows — every `α_s < 1`, so multiplying in one more always pulls it down further.

### Step 3 — √ᾱ_t and √(1−ᾱ_t)
From the closed form `x_t = √ᾱ_t · x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_t) · ε`, `√ᾱ_t` is the weight on the original signal `x_0` and `√(1−ᾱ_t)` is the weight on the injected noise `ε`.

| t | ᾱ_t | √ᾱ_t | 1−ᾱ_t | √(1−ᾱ_t) | √ᾱ_t / √(1−ᾱ_t) |
|---|------|-------|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1 | 0.900 | 0.9487 | 0.100 | 0.3162 | 3.000 |
| 2 | 0.720 | 0.8485 | 0.280 | 0.5292 | 1.603 |
| 3 | 0.504 | 0.7099 | 0.496 | 0.7043 | 1.008 |

(Check at t=1: `0.9/0.1 = 9`, and `√9 = 3` exactly, so no rounding slop there.)

### Analysis — signal vs noise across steps
The ratio `√ᾱ_t / √(1−ᾱ_t)` is effectively the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** of `x_t`. In this toy schedule it falls from `3.0` at t=1 to about `1.0` by t=3 — by the third step the signal term and the noise term are contributing almost equally to `x_t`. Two things worth carrying forward:
- `ᾱ_t` is monotonically decreasing in t, always — it's a product of numbers each less than 1, so it can never go back up.
- A real DDPM schedule (e.g. linear `β` from `1e-4` to `0.02` over `T = 1000` steps) decays far more gently per step than this fast 3-step toy example — SNR only approaches 0 near the final timestep, by which point `x_T` is, by design, indistinguishable from pure Gaussian noise `N(0, I)`.

## Exam tip
If the paper gives actual `β` values, repeat the exact three steps in order — `α_t` → `ᾱ_t` (cumulative **product**, not sum) → the two square roots. The most common mistake is summing `β_t` instead of multiplying `(1−β_t)` terms; always convert to `α_t` first, then take the running product.
