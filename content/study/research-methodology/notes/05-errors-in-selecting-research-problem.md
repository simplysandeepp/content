## The idea, in plain words

Picking a research problem is easy to get wrong in predictable ways. Knowing the common **errors**
helps a researcher avoid wasting months on a problem that was never going to work — and it's a
direct flip side of topic 4 (Characteristics of a Good Research Problem): most of these errors are
simply what happens when one of those good characteristics is missing.

## Common errors in selecting a research problem

- **Too broad or too vague** — the problem can't actually be investigated within real time, budget,
  or data constraints because it tries to cover too much ground.
- **Not researchable** — the problem is really a value judgement ("is X better than Y?"), not
  something data can settle empirically.
- **Already thoroughly studied** — the problem lacks originality; it just repeats a settled answer
  instead of adding anything new.
- **Beyond the researcher's competence** — the problem demands skills, domain knowledge, or
  resources (equipment, funding, field access) the researcher doesn't actually have.
- **Ambiguous problem statement** — the problem itself is worded unclearly, so different people
  reading it would understand it differently.
- **Feasibility never checked** — time, cost, and data-access constraints weren't verified before
  committing to the problem, only discovered partway through.
- **Personal bias shaped the choice** — the problem was picked because of the researcher's own
  preference or agenda rather than a genuine need or gap.
- **Doesn't match the researcher's real interest** — even a technically sound problem hurts
  motivation and the quality of the work over a long study if the researcher isn't genuinely
  interested in it.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q6: "Examine the common errors while selecting a research problem, and suggest measures to minimize such errors."

**Answer plan:** list each error above, pairing it with one concrete fix:

- Too broad/vague → narrow the scope with clear boundaries early (define population, time period,
  and variables up front).
- Not researchable → reframe the question so it's measurable and empirical, not a value judgement.
- Already studied → do a proper literature review first to confirm the gap genuinely exists.
- Beyond competence → honestly assess the required skills and resources before committing, and seek
  co-researchers or training if there's a gap.
- Ambiguous statement → write and test the problem statement for clarity, ideally getting a second
  reader to check it means one single thing.
- Feasibility not checked → run a feasibility check (time, cost, data access) before finalising the
  problem, not after starting.
- Personal bias → consult peers/mentors to sanity-check that the problem reflects a genuine need, not
  just the researcher's own preference.
- Doesn't match real interest → choose among feasible, valid problems the one the researcher is
  genuinely motivated by, since long studies need sustained motivation.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q3: "Differentiate between quantitative and qualitative errors in selecting a research problem, and analyze their impact on the validity of research findings."

**Answer plan:** this question's wording isn't rigidly standardised in the literature, so state
plainly that what follows is one clear, reasoned framing of the distinction, then present it:

- **Quantitative errors** — errors about scope and measurability: the problem is too broad, too
  narrow, has unmeasurable variables, or the researcher has insufficient sample size or data access.
  Impact on validity: these tend to make results **statistically unreliable or inconclusive** — the
  numbers either can't be collected properly or don't carry enough statistical power to support a
  real conclusion.
- **Qualitative errors** — errors about judgement and framing: ambiguous wording, personal bias
  shaping the problem, ethical problems with the study, or poor alignment between the stated problem
  and the real underlying need. Impact on validity: these tend to produce results that may look
  statistically fine on the surface but are **meaningless, misdirected, or biased in interpretation**
  — the data collection process itself worked, but it was answering the wrong or a distorted
  question.

Close by noting the key practical difference: quantitative errors usually show up as a study that
can't produce usable numbers at all, while qualitative errors are more dangerous because they can
produce numbers that look valid but are actually answering a poorly framed or biased version of the
real problem.
