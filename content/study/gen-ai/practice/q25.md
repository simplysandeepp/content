## What's actually being asked
Describe how G and D are built layer by layer — inputs, outputs, activations — and trace exactly what happens to the data and the gradients during a single training iteration.

## Answer

### Generator G
- **Input**: a random noise vector `z ∼ p_z(z)`, usually `z ∼ N(0, I)`, dimension e.g. 100.
- **Body**: a stack of fully-connected layers (vanilla GAN) or transposed-convolution layers (DCGAN-style) that progressively upsample `z` towards the shape of real data.
- **Hidden activations**: ReLU or LeakyReLU — keeps gradients flowing through the stack without saturating too early.
- **Output layer**: shape matches real data; activation is **Tanh** if real data is scaled to [-1,1], or **Sigmoid** if scaled to [0,1].
- **Output**: `G(z)` — a fake sample living in data space.

### Discriminator D
- **Input**: a sample `x`, either a real one (from `p_data`) or a fake one (`G(z)`).
- **Body**: fully-connected layers (vanilla) or convolution layers (DCGAN) that progressively compress the input down to a single scalar.
- **Hidden activations**: **LeakyReLU** is preferred over plain ReLU — avoids dead units and keeps gradient alive on both sides of the activation, important since D has to stay sensitive to subtle real-vs-fake differences.
- **Output layer**: a single neuron with **Sigmoid** activation → `D(x) ∈ (0,1)`, interpreted as the estimated probability that `x` is real.

### Diagram
See the reference diagram: noise flows through G into a fake sample; both the fake sample and a real sample flow into the *same* D instance, which outputs one real/fake score.

### Information flow during one training iteration
1. Sample a batch of real data `x ∼ p_data` and a batch of noise `z ∼ p_z`.
2. Forward `z` through G → fake batch `G(z)`.
3. Forward both the real batch and the fake batch through D → `D(x)` and `D(G(z))`.
4. Compute D's loss, `−[log D(x) + log(1 − D(G(z)))]`; backpropagate; update **D's weights only** (G is frozen for this step).
5. Sample a fresh noise batch `z`, forward through G, then through the just-updated D again to get `D(G(z))`.
6. Compute G's loss (non-saturating form used in practice), `−log D(G(z))`; backpropagate the gradient through D's weights into G, but update **G's weights only** (D is frozen for this step).
7. Repeat steps 1–6 for many iterations.

## Exam tip
The diagram carries real marks here — always show noise as G's only input, one shared D instance taking both real and fake samples, and state explicitly that G's weight update requires a forward (and backward) pass through D while D's own weights stay frozen during that step. Examiners frequently probe this freeze/update split.
