## What's actually being asked
GAN architecture and G-D interaction, traced through a forward pass and a backward pass, plus why the latent-space input to G matters.

## Answer

### Architecture
- **Generator G** (params θ_g): input is latent noise z, dimension typically much smaller than the output data (e.g., 100-D z for a 64×64 image). Built from fully-connected or transposed-convolutional layers with upsampling, batch norm, and ReLU/LeakyReLU activations, ending in a tanh or sigmoid output matching the data's value range.
- **Discriminator D** (params θ_d): input is a data-shaped sample (real or fake); built from convolutional layers with LeakyReLU and downsampling, ending in a single sigmoid unit giving `D(·)∈(0,1)`.

### Forward pass
1. Sample `z ~ p_z` (e.g., `N(0,I)`).
2. `x_fake = G(z)` — forward through G.
3. Sample a real batch `x ~ p_data`.
4. Both `x_fake` and `x` are forwarded through D, producing `D(G(z))` and `D(x)`.
5. These feed the value function: `V(D,G) = 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[log D(x)] + 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[log(1−D(G(z)))]`.

(See the diagram — the forward-pass edges trace exactly this path: z → G → fake sample → D ← real sample → loss.)

### Backward pass
Two separate backward passes happen, not one joint one:
- **D's backward pass**: gradient of `V(D,G)` taken w.r.t. `θ_d` only (G's weights treated as constant for this step), and `θ_d` is updated by **gradient ascent** (D wants to *maximize* V) — `θ_d ← θ_d + η∇_{θ_d} V`.
- **G's backward pass**: gradient taken w.r.t. `θ_g` only, through D's (now fixed) weights back into G — this is why D must be differentiable end-to-end, since the gradient signal for G has to pass *through* D. G is updated by gradient descent on `𝔼[log(1−D(G(z)))]` (or ascent on the non-saturating `𝔼[log D(G(z))]`) — `θ_g ← θ_g − η∇_{θ_g}[log(1−D(G(z)))]`.

This alternation (the diagram's two backward edges, both flowing out of the loss node) is what makes it a minimax game rather than a single end-to-end supervised model — the loss is shared, but the update direction is opposite for each player on their own parameters.

### Significance of the latent-space input
z is the *only* source of randomness/diversity in the entire system — without it, G would be a deterministic function producing one fixed output. Key properties this input gives the architecture:
- **Diversity of generation**: different draws of z should map to different, diverse samples covering the breadth of `p_data`, not collapsing to a handful of outputs (mode collapse is exactly this property breaking down).
- **Structured, continuous space**: because G is a smooth (differentiable) function, nearby points in z-space tend to decode to visually similar outputs, so the latent space supports meaningful interpolation (walking from `z_1` to `z_2` produces a smooth morph) and, in practice, sometimes exposes semantically meaningful directions (e.g., correlating with pose or style) even though nothing explicitly supervised that structure.
- **Decoupling from data at inference**: once trained, generation needs no real data at all — just fresh `z ~ p_z` through G — which is what makes GANs usable as standalone generators, unlike models that need an encoder/input at inference time.
- **Dimensionality as a design choice**: too low a dimension limits how much variation G can express; too high can make training harder to stabilize and the space harder to fill meaningfully — choosing z's dimensionality is an explicit architecture decision, not incidental.

## Exam tip
If asked about "forward and backward passes" specifically, explicitly say that G's backward pass gradient has to flow *through* D's frozen weights — that single detail (gradient relay through a frozen network) is what examiners most often probe to check real understanding versus a memorized diagram.
