## The idea, in plain words

Before starting any new research, a researcher reads what's already been written on the topic. This
is the **literature review**. Its job is simple to state but easy to do badly: find out what's
already known, spot the gaps, make sure you're not accidentally repeating someone else's study, and
build the theoretical foundation the new study will stand on.

## Why do a literature review at all

- **Identify what's already known** — so the new study adds something, instead of re-discovering a
  settled answer.
- **Spot gaps and contradictions** — these gaps are very often where a genuinely good research
  problem comes from (see "3. Sources of Research Problem").
- **Avoid duplicating existing work** — wasted effort if someone already answered the exact same
  question.
- **Build the theoretical foundation** — the concepts, definitions, and prior findings the new study
  will build its own argument on top of.

## Two main approaches

- **Narrative review** — a broad, descriptive summary of relevant work. Flexible structure, no fixed
  protocol, written more like a guided tour of the topic. Faster to produce, but more subjective and
  harder for someone else to reproduce exactly, since the choice of what to include is left to the
  reviewer's own judgement.
- **Systematic review** — follows a strict, predefined, and fully documented protocol for how studies
  are searched for, selected, and evaluated. Far more rigorous and reproducible, because another
  researcher following the same documented protocol should arrive at the same set of included
  studies. This is the standard expected in evidence-based fields (like medicine) where the review
  itself is treated as a piece of research in its own right.

## The process, step by step

1. **Define the review question** — what exactly is this review trying to find out.
2. **Search databases with defined keywords** — using academic databases and a clear, repeatable
   search strategy.
3. **Screen and select relevant sources** against clear inclusion/exclusion criteria.
4. **Critically appraise** each source's quality — methodology, sample size, potential bias,
   relevance.
5. **Synthesise findings across sources** — look for patterns, agreements, and contradictions between
   studies, not just summaries of each one in isolation.
6. **Identify the gap** the new research will fill — the whole point of doing all of the above.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 3 (literature/data) Q1: "Evaluate the different literature study approaches and justify which is most suitable for a systematic review."

**Answer plan:** describe both the narrative and systematic approaches as above, then answer the
"justify" part directly: for a systematic review specifically, the systematic approach is the only
suitable choice — almost by definition, since a systematic review *requires* the documented,
reproducible search-and-selection protocol described above. Contrast this against why a narrative
approach would be the weaker choice there: its flexible, judgement-driven selection process is exactly
what a systematic review is designed to eliminate, so using it would undermine the rigour the
systematic review is meant to provide.

### Practice set, Unit 3 Q2: "Critique the process of conducting an effective literature review and propose improvements to it."

**Answer plan:** go through the six process steps above and critique each — for example, a keyword
search alone can easily miss relevant work that uses different terminology for the same concept
(propose: searching multiple databases, and adding snowball/citation-chasing search — following
references forward and backward from known key papers — to cover what keyword search misses);
screening against inclusion/exclusion criteria can still be subjective (propose: having two
reviewers screen independently and compare); critical appraisal quality depends entirely on the
reviewer's own expertise (propose: using a standard appraisal checklist rather than ad hoc judgement).

### Practice set, Unit 3 Q5: "Judge the strengths and limitations of narrative review versus systematic review approaches."

**Answer plan:** narrative review — strength: flexible, fast, good for broadly exploring a topic
that doesn't yet have a tightly defined question; limitation: subjective, hard for someone else to
reproduce, carries a real risk of selective citation (unconsciously favouring sources that support
a particular view). Systematic review — strength: rigorous, reproducible, specifically designed to
minimise selection bias; limitation: time-consuming and resource-heavy, and its strict protocol can
sometimes be too narrow for a genuinely exploratory topic where the right question hasn't been
pinned down yet. Judge that the right choice depends on where the research actually is: exploring
a new area favours narrative, confirming or synthesising evidence on a well-defined question favours
systematic.

### Real mid-term, Q8 (K4, 8 marks): "Sketch a detailed plan for conducting effective literature studies approaches. Your plan should demonstrate an understanding of the key steps and techniques in conducting literature studies."

**Answer plan:** this is a K4/8-mark "sketch a plan" question, so go one level more concrete than the
six-step process above — name an actual technique at each stage, not just the stage name:

1. **Define the review question** — write it as a specific, answerable question (not a broad topic).
2. **Search** — use multiple academic databases (not just one), with a documented keyword list and
   Boolean search operators (AND/OR/NOT) to combine terms precisely.
3. **Select** — apply explicit, written inclusion/exclusion criteria (e.g. publication date range,
   study type, language) agreed *before* screening starts, so selection isn't made up as you go.
4. **Critically appraise** — score each included source against a fixed quality checklist
   (methodology soundness, sample size, potential bias, relevance to the question).
5. **Track citations** — use forward and backward citation-chasing (snowballing) from key papers to
   catch relevant work the keyword search alone missed (see topic 13 for why this matters).
6. **Synthesise** — organise findings into a synthesis matrix or thematic table so patterns,
   agreements, and contradictions across sources are visible at a glance, not just summarised one
   source at a time.
7. **Identify and state the gap** — the explicit output of the whole plan, which becomes the
   justification for the new research problem.

Naming a concrete technique at each step (Boolean operators, a written criteria list, a quality
checklist, snowballing, a synthesis matrix) is what turns this into a genuine "plan" rather than a
restatement of the process — that's specifically what the question is testing at K4.
