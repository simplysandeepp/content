## What's actually being asked
State the minimax value function, split it into what D maximizes and what G minimizes (or maximizes, in the practical variant), and explain why this pairing is what makes the "adversarial" training actually work.

## Answer

### The minimax value function
`min_G max_D V(D,G) = 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[log D(x)] + 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[log(1 − D(G(z)))]`

Symbols: `D(x)` — discriminator's estimated probability that `x` is real; `G(z)` — generator's fake sample produced from noise `z`; `p_data` — the real-data distribution; `p_z` — the noise prior (usually `N(0,I)`).

### Discriminator loss
D maximizes: `log D(x) + log(1 − D(G(z)))`.
In practice this is implemented as binary cross-entropy — label real samples `1`, fake samples `0` — minimizing
`L_D = −[log D(x) + log(1 − D(G(z)))]`.
D wants `D(x) → 1` on real data and `D(G(z)) → 0` on fakes: a straightforward binary classifier.

### Generator loss
Two standard forms:
- **Saturating** (original minimax): G minimizes `log(1 − D(G(z)))`.
- **Non-saturating** (used in practice): G maximizes `log D(G(z))`, i.e. minimizes `−log D(G(z))`.

Why the non-saturating form is preferred: early in training D easily tells real from fake, so `D(G(z)) ≈ 0`. At that point `log(1 − D(G(z)))` sits in a flat region of its curve — tiny gradient, G barely learns. `−log D(G(z))` is steep exactly when `D(G(z))` is small, so it gives G a strong learning signal early on, when it needs it most.

### How these losses drive the adversarial process
Matlab, training is a two-player game played with alternating gradient steps:
- D is trained to be the best possible classifier against the *current* G.
- G is trained to best fool the *current* D.
- D's gradient tells G which directions in data space currently look "obviously fake" — G nudges its output distribution `p_g` to erase exactly those tells.
- As this repeats, `p_g` is pushed toward `p_data`. At the theoretical equilibrium (Nash equilibrium of the minimax game), `p_g = p_data` and D can do no better than random guessing — `D(x) = 1/2` everywhere, for both real and fake inputs.
- The key coupling: each network's loss is a direct function of the *other* network's current output. Neither can improve its loss in isolation without implicitly forcing a response from the other — that mutual dependency is the whole mechanism behind "adversarial" training, as opposed to two networks trained independently.

## Exam tip
Always write both the saturating and non-saturating Generator loss, and explicitly justify which one is used in practice and why (the flat-gradient region of `log(1−d)` near `d≈0`). Stating only one form, or stating both without the justification, is a common way to lose marks here.
