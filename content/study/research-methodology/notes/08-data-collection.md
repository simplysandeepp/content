## The idea, in plain words

**Data** is the raw facts, figures, or observations a researcher gathers to investigate the research
problem — it's the evidence everything else (analysis, interpretation, conclusions) is built on.
Data can be split along more than one dimension, but the exam-relevant split is about *where the
data comes from*: did the researcher collect it themselves for this exact study (primary), or is it
something someone else already collected for a different purpose (secondary)? There's also the
numbers-vs-descriptions split: **quantitative data** is numerical (e.g. "62% of respondents agreed"),
while **qualitative data** is descriptive, non-numerical information about qualities or experiences
(e.g. "respondents said the new interface felt confusing").

## Primary data

Collected **firsthand by the researcher**, specifically for the study at hand. Common methods:

- **Questionnaires / surveys** — structured questions sent to a sample of respondents.
- **Interviews** — structured (fixed questions, easy to compare across respondents) or unstructured
  (open conversation, more depth but harder to compare).
- **Direct observation** — watching and recording behaviour as it happens, without relying on
  self-report.
- **Experiments** — manipulating a variable under controlled conditions to observe the effect.

**Trade-off:** primary data is more relevant and current — it's built exactly around this study's
own questions — but it's costlier and more time-consuming to collect than reusing existing data.

## Secondary data

Data that was **already collected by someone else, for a different original purpose** — books,
journal articles, government reports, existing databases, past survey datasets.

**Trade-off:** secondary data is cheaper and faster to access since the collection work is already
done, but it may not perfectly fit the new research question (wrong population, wrong time period,
wrong variables measured), and its original quality or bias can't always be verified by the new
researcher.

## Choosing a primary data collection method

Even once "go primary" is decided, the specific method still matters, and the right one depends on
the scenario:

- **Questionnaire** — cheap, scales to large samples easily, but gives less depth per respondent
  (fixed questions can't chase an interesting answer further).
- **Interview** — gives rich, detailed information and lets the researcher probe follow-up answers,
  but is time-costly per respondent and harder to run at scale.
- **Observation** — captures what people actually do (real behaviour), but can't capture the
  motives or reasons behind that behaviour — the researcher has to infer those, or pair observation
  with another method.

The right choice depends on the specific research scenario's goals: breadth and speed point toward
questionnaires, depth and nuance point toward interviews, and verifying actual behaviour (as opposed
to what people say they do) points toward observation.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Real mid-term, Q4 (K2, 4 marks): "Define data and its types with appropriate examples."

**Answer plan:** at K2/4 marks this wants a clear definition-plus-examples paragraph, not a long
essay. First define data (the raw facts, figures, or observations gathered as evidence for a
research problem). Then give the **primary/secondary** split with one example each (primary: a
researcher's own customer survey; secondary: a government census report reused for a new study), and
the **quantitative/qualitative** split with one example each (quantitative: age or income figures;
qualitative: a respondent's written description of their experience). Four types, four short
examples — that's the whole answer at this mark weight.

### Real mid-term, Q5 (K3, 5 marks): "Contrast between primary and secondary data collection methods in research. Give examples of situations where each method would be most suitable."

**Answer plan:** build a short contrast covering source, cost, time, relevance, and control over
quality:
- **Source** — primary is collected firsthand by the researcher; secondary was collected by someone
  else for a different purpose.
- **Cost and time** — primary is costlier and slower to collect; secondary is cheaper and faster
  since it already exists.
- **Relevance** — primary fits the exact research question perfectly since it was designed for it;
  secondary may only approximately fit.
- **Control over quality** — the researcher fully controls primary data's quality and method;
  secondary data's original quality and any bias in it can't always be verified.

Then give one concrete "when to use" example for each, as the question explicitly asks:
**primary** is most suitable for a brand-new question nobody has studied yet — e.g. a company
directly surveying its own customers about a new product feature. **Secondary** is most suitable for
a quick trend analysis using data that already exists in the right form — e.g. using existing
government census data to study population trends, where collecting brand-new data would be
redundant and wasteful.

### Practice set, Unit 3 (literature/data) Q8: "Assess the suitability of different primary data collection methods for a given research scenario."

**Answer plan:** briefly compare the three primary methods as above — **questionnaire** (cheap,
scalable, less depth), **interview** (rich detail, time-costly), **observation** (captures real
behaviour, can't capture motives or reasons) — then note explicitly that the right choice depends on
the specific research scenario's own goals: if the priority is reaching a large sample quickly and
cheaply, choose questionnaires; if the priority is deep understanding of a smaller group's reasoning,
choose interviews; if the priority is verifying actual behaviour rather than self-reported behaviour,
choose observation. A strong answer picks one example scenario and justifies which method fits it
best, rather than just repeating the three definitions.
