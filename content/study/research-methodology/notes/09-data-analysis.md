## The idea, in plain words

Once data is collected, it's still just raw material — **data analysis** is the process of
organising, summarising, and examining that data to find patterns in it. There are two broad
families of analysis, and they answer two different questions.

## Descriptive analysis

Summarises **what the data shows**, for the sample that was actually collected. It answers: "what's
the pattern in this sample?"

Typical tools:
- **Mean, median, mode** — central-tendency measures that summarise a "typical" value.
- **Frequency counts** — how often each value or category occurs.
- **Charts and tables** — visual summaries that make the pattern easy to see at a glance.

Descriptive analysis never claims anything about people or cases outside the sample itself — it's
purely a summary of the data in hand.

## Inferential analysis

Uses the sample data to make a **generalisation or prediction about a larger population** beyond
just the sample collected. It answers: "what's likely true beyond this sample?"

Typical tools:
- **Hypothesis testing** — checking whether an observed effect in the sample is likely to be real in
  the wider population, or just due to chance.
- **Confidence intervals** — a range within which the true population value is likely to fall.
- **Correlation** — whether two variables move together.
- **Regression** — modelling how one variable predicts or affects another.

Inferential analysis is what lets a study say something about people or cases it never actually
collected data from — but that leap is only valid if the sample was properly selected and the
relevant statistical assumptions hold.

## How they relate

Descriptive analysis is the necessary first step — it's how a researcher actually understands their
own dataset before trying to claim anything bigger about it. Inferential analysis is the step that
turns a sample-level summary into a defensible claim about the wider population the sample was drawn
from. Skipping straight to inferential analysis without first understanding the data descriptively
is how misleading or poorly-checked conclusions happen.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 3 (literature/data) Q11: "Judge the effectiveness of descriptive versus inferential analysis in drawing research conclusions."

**Answer plan:** since the question says "judge," give a real verdict, not just two definitions.
**Descriptive analysis** is effective for simply understanding and reporting the sample itself —
mean, median, frequency counts, and charts give a clear, honest picture of what was actually
collected — but it can't, by itself, support any claim about people or cases outside that sample.
**Inferential analysis** is needed whenever the research wants to generalise its findings to a wider
population — hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, correlation, and regression are what make that
leap possible — but inferential analysis is only as trustworthy as the sampling behind it: proper
random/representative sampling and the right statistical assumptions have to hold, or the inference
is invalid even if the maths is done correctly. Judge that **most rigorous research needs both,
used in sequence**: descriptive analysis first, to actually understand what the data shows, and
inferential analysis second, to draw the real, generalisable research conclusion the study is aiming
for. Neither one alone is sufficient — descriptive without inferential can't generalise, and
inferential without first understanding the data descriptively risks drawing conclusions nobody has
actually sanity-checked.
