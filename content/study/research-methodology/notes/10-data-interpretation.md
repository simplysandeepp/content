## The idea, in plain words

Analysis gives you numbers and patterns. **Interpretation** is the step after that: figuring out
what those numbers actually *mean*, in plain language, and tying them back to the original research
question. Two studies can run the exact same statistical test and still "interpret" the result
differently depending on how carefully they connect it back to the real-world question they set out
to answer. Interpretation is where a pile of statistics turns into an actual finding.

## What interpretation means, specifically

- It **explains the pattern** the analysis found — not just "mean = 42," but what a mean of 42
  actually implies about the thing being studied.
- It **connects back to the research objectives and hypothesis** — every interpreted finding should
  answer, directly, one of the questions the study set out to ask. A result that doesn't map back to
  the original problem is interesting trivia, not a research finding.
- It's the bridge between the data and the conclusions/recommendations section of a report — nothing
  after this point should introduce a claim the data itself doesn't actually support.

## Precautions while interpreting data

- **Don't over-generalise** — a finding only applies to what the sample actually represents. Claiming
  a result holds for "all students" when the sample was 50 students from one college is a classic
  over-generalisation error.
- **Check for alternative explanations and confounding variables** — before accepting the obvious
  explanation, ask whether something else could have produced the same pattern.
- **Distinguish correlation from causation** — two things moving together does not mean one causes
  the other. This is the single most common interpretation mistake in research.
- **Watch for the researcher's own bias** — when a result is ambiguous, it's tempting to read it in
  the direction you expected or hoped for. Good interpretation stays honest about what's actually
  ambiguous.
- **Cross-check against existing literature** — if a finding is wildly inconsistent with what prior
  studies found, that's a signal to double-check the analysis before reporting it as a breakthrough.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 3 (literature/data) Q9: "Critique the techniques and precautions used in interpreting research data."

**Answer plan:** list the five precautions above, then critique each one specifically rather than
just restating it — for example, "avoid over-generalising" is easy to say but hard to enforce in
practice unless the sample's scope was clearly defined *before* data collection started; "distinguish
correlation from causation" requires the researcher to actively design the study (or at least the
analysis) to rule out alternative explanations, not just remember the rule while writing up results;
"watch for researcher bias" is hardest of all to self-police, which is why peer review and
independent replication exist as outside checks. Close by noting that good interpretation always
ties numbers back to the specific research question — presenting statistics in isolation, without
connecting them to what the study was actually trying to find out, is the core failure interpretation
exists to prevent.
