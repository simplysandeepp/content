## The idea, in plain words

A **research report** is the finished, written account of a completed piece of research — what the
problem was, what was done, what was found, and what it means. Writing one well isn't just about
getting the content right; it's about organising it so a reader (an examiner, a supervisor, another
researcher) can follow the work, trust it, and reuse it.

## Types of research reports

- **Technical report** — written for a specialist or scientific audience. It's formal and detailed:
  full methodology, complete data, statistical detail, and technical terminology the audience is
  assumed to already understand.
- **Popular report** — written for a general, non-specialist audience. It's simplified: it focuses on
  the findings and what they mean in practice, uses minimal jargon, and usually drops or shrinks the
  full methodology section since that audience doesn't need (or want) that level of technical detail.

## Steps in writing a research report

1. **Logical analysis of the subject and a rough outline** — work out the main sections the report
   needs and the logical order they should go in, before writing any actual prose.
2. **Preparation of the final, detailed outline** — turn the rough outline into a fuller, more
   specific plan: what exactly goes under each heading.
3. **Preparation of the rough draft** — write the first full version, following the detailed outline,
   without worrying too much yet about polish.
4. **Rewriting and polishing the draft** — revise for clarity, accuracy, flow, and the 4 C's (clear,
   concise, correct, complete) from the technical-writing topic — this step is usually done more than
   once.
5. **Preparing the final bibliography** — compile the complete, correctly formatted list of every
   source cited in the report.
6. **Writing the final, clean draft** — produce the finished version that incorporates all the
   revisions and the finished bibliography, ready for submission.

## Layout of a research report

A standard research report is organised into sections, each with its own job for the reader:

- **Title page** — names the study, the author, the institution, and the date.
- **Acknowledgements** — credits anyone (supervisors, funders, participants) who helped the work
  happen.
- **Table of contents** — lets the reader navigate straight to any section.
- **Abstract / executive summary** — a short, self-contained summary of the whole report: problem,
  method, key findings, and conclusion, so a reader can grasp the essentials without reading further.
- **Introduction** — states the research problem, its background, and why it matters.
- **Review of literature** — summarises existing relevant work and shows the gap this study addresses.
- **Methodology** — explains exactly how the research was conducted: design, sample, data
  collection, and analysis method, in enough detail that another researcher could judge or repeat it.
- **Results / findings** — presents what was actually found, usually with tables, charts, or
  statistics, without yet interpreting what it means.
- **Discussion** — interprets the findings, connects them back to the research problem and existing
  literature, and discusses their implications.
- **Conclusions and recommendations** — states the final answer to the research problem and any
  practical recommendations that follow from it.
- **References / bibliography** — the full, properly formatted list of every source cited.
- **Appendices** — supporting material that's too detailed for the main body (raw data, full
  questionnaires, extra tables) but useful for a reader who wants to verify or dig deeper.

## Precautions while preparing a research report

- Stay **concise yet complete** — include everything necessary, nothing that isn't.
- **Organise logically**, following the standard layout above, so the reader is never lost.
- Stay **free of personal bias** — report what the data actually shows, not what the researcher
  hoped or expected to find.
- **Cite properly throughout** — every claim or data point drawn from elsewhere needs a citation
  (this directly overlaps with avoiding plagiarism, covered in topic 14).
- **Proofread thoroughly** — careless errors undermine the reader's trust in the report's accuracy.
- **Write for the actual intended audience** — match the level of technical detail to who will
  actually read it (technical vs. popular report, as above).
- **Never state a generalisation the data doesn't actually support** — conclusions should stay
  within what the evidence genuinely justifies.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q6: "Identify the different types of technical reports and name their general outline."

**Answer plan:** identify the two report types — technical report (specialist audience, full
methodology and data) and popular report (general audience, findings-focused, minimal jargon) — then
give the general outline shared by both: title page, acknowledgements, table of contents,
abstract/executive summary, introduction, review of literature, methodology, results, discussion,
conclusions and recommendations, references, appendices (noting a popular report typically shrinks or
drops the full methodology section).

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q7: "Explain the different steps involved in writing a research report."

**Answer plan:** walk through the six steps above in order — logical analysis and rough outline,
final detailed outline, rough draft, rewriting and polishing, final bibliography, final clean draft —
explaining briefly what each step accomplishes and why the order matters (you can't polish a draft
that doesn't exist yet; you can't finalise a bibliography before you know what's actually been cited
in the final text).

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q8: "Describe the layout of a research report and outline the contents included in each section."

**Answer plan:** go through the full layout above, section by section, giving one line on what each
section specifically contains: title page (study name, author, institution, date), acknowledgements
(credits to helpers/funders), table of contents (navigation), abstract (self-contained summary),
introduction (problem and background), review of literature (existing work and the gap), methodology
(design, sample, data collection, analysis), results (what was found), discussion (interpretation and
implications), conclusions and recommendations (final answer and practical suggestions), references
(full source list), appendices (supporting detail).

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q9: "Discuss the statement 'Report writing is more an art that hinges upon practice & experience,' and narrate the various steps involved in writing such a report."

**Answer plan:** agree with the statement and explain why — the structural rules (layout, citation
format) can be taught in an afternoon, but the actual judgement a good report needs (what to include
versus leave for an appendix, how to phrase a finding precisely without overstating it, how to
balance completeness against concision) only sharpens with repeated practice, feedback, and
experience — two researchers can follow the identical outline and produce reports of very different
quality. Then narrate the six writing steps above as the "steps involved."

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q10: "Explain the different types of reports and describe the general outline of a technical report."

**Answer plan:** explain both report types (technical vs. popular, as above), then focus specifically
on the technical report's outline: title page, acknowledgements, table of contents, abstract,
introduction, review of literature, full methodology, results, discussion, conclusions and
recommendations, references, appendices — emphasising that the technical report keeps the full
methodology and technical detail that a popular report would simplify away.

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q11: "Describe the difference between a technical report and a popular report, giving their respective outlines."

**Answer plan:** give a direct comparison across: **audience** (specialists vs. general readers),
**depth of methodology/data shown** (full and detailed vs. minimal or summarised), **language/jargon
level** (technical terminology assumed vs. plain language), and **typical outline differences** (a
popular report often drops or shrinks the full methodology section and leads with
findings/implications instead, while the technical report keeps the complete standard layout). Then
give each report's own outline side by side using the layout list above, trimmed for the popular
report.

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q12: "Discuss the precautions a researcher should keep in mind while preparing a good research report."

**Answer plan:** discuss each precaution above — stay concise yet complete, organise logically
following the layout, stay free of personal bias, cite properly throughout, proofread thoroughly,
write for the actual audience, and never generalise beyond what the data supports — giving one
sentence on why each matters for the report's quality and credibility.
