## The idea, in plain words

If topic 18 was about *why* a research proposal exists and what it needs to contain, this topic is
about the actual **section-by-section structure** a proposal follows — the specific order a reviewer
expects to read things in, and what each section is there to let that reviewer judge.

## The typical section order, and what each section is for

- **Title** — concise and descriptive, telling the reviewer at a glance what the study is about.
  A vague or overly clever title makes the reviewer work harder before they've even started reading.
- **Introduction / Background** — states the context and the problem the study addresses. Its role
  is to let the reviewer quickly understand *why this problem, why now* before they get into any
  detail.
- **Objectives / Research questions** — the clear, measurable goals the research aims to achieve.
  Its role is to give the reviewer a concrete standard to judge the rest of the proposal against: does
  the proposed methodology actually look capable of achieving these stated objectives?
- **Literature review** — a brief review demonstrating the gap the study will address. Its role is to
  show the reviewer the researcher already knows the existing field well enough to be sure this isn't
  just repeating settled work.
- **Research methodology** — the design, sample, data collection, and analysis plan. This is the
  section that specifically exists so a reviewer can judge whether the plan is actually **feasible
  and rigorous**, not just whether the topic sounds interesting — it's usually the section a strong
  reviewer scrutinises hardest.
- **Significance / expected contribution** — states what the study is expected to find or contribute,
  and why that matters. Its role is to justify, for the reviewer, that the result (if achieved) is
  actually worth the resources being requested.
- **Timeline / work plan** — often shown as a schedule or Gantt-style chart. Its role is to let the
  reviewer judge whether the proposed work can realistically be completed in the time available or
  requested.
- **Budget**, where relevant — its role is to let a funding body judge whether the requested
  resources match the scope of the proposed work.
- **References / Bibliography** — the full list of sources cited, letting the reviewer verify the
  literature review's claims and see the proposal is properly grounded in existing work.

## Why the format itself matters

The whole point of following a standard, predictable format is that it lets a reviewer evaluate a
proposal efficiently and fairly — they know exactly where to look to check the problem, the
objectives, the feasibility of the method, and the realism of the timeline, rather than having to
hunt for that information inside an unstructured document. A proposal that skips or buries one of
these sections (most commonly a weak or vague methodology section) is the most common reason a
proposal gets rejected or sent back for revision, even when the underlying research idea itself is
sound.
