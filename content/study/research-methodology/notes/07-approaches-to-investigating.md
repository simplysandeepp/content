## The idea, in plain words

Once a research problem and its scope/objectives are set, the researcher has to decide *how* to go
about actually investigating it. There are three broad approaches, and the right one depends on
whether the question needs hard numbers, rich human detail, or both.

## Quantitative approach

Investigates the problem through **numerical data** and statistical analysis.

- Uses larger samples, aiming for results that can be **generalised** to a wider population.
- Tests a specific, pre-defined hypothesis.
- Relies on objective, standardised instruments (e.g. structured questionnaires with fixed-choice
  answers) so that results from different respondents can be directly compared and statistically
  analysed.
- Good fit when the research question is something like "how much," "how many," or "is there a
  measurable relationship between X and Y."

## Qualitative approach

Investigates the problem through **non-numerical data** — interviews, direct observation, case
studies, open-ended discussion.

- Uses small samples, because the goal is depth, not breadth.
- Explores meaning, experience, and context in detail; it's **interpretive** rather than purely
  objective — the researcher's own careful reading of the material is part of the method.
- Not meant to produce results that generalise to everyone; it's meant to understand a phenomenon
  richly, in its own context.
- Good fit for "why" and "how" questions, or when the researcher doesn't yet know enough about the
  phenomenon to even write a fixed-choice questionnaire about it.

## Pragmatic / mixed-methods approach

Combines quantitative and qualitative methods **within the same study**.

- The choice isn't made out of philosophical loyalty to one paradigm — it's made because the
  specific research question genuinely needs both the breadth that quantitative data gives and the
  depth that qualitative data gives to be properly answered.
- Typical pattern: a quantitative survey establishes *what* is happening at scale, and a smaller set
  of qualitative interviews explains *why* it's happening, or vice versa.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 3 (literature/data) Q7: "Evaluate the quantitative, qualitative, and pragmatic approaches to investigating a research problem."

**Answer plan:** describe all three approaches using the detail above — for each, cover what kind
of data it uses, sample size, and the kind of question it suits. Then actually *evaluate* them
against a few criteria, since the question says "evaluate," not just "describe":
- **Generalisability** — quantitative is strong here (large samples, statistical testing);
  qualitative is weak (small samples, context-specific); pragmatic can achieve some of both.
- **Depth of insight** — qualitative is strongest (rich, contextual understanding); quantitative is
  weakest on its own (numbers without the "why"); pragmatic captures both breadth and depth.
- **Cost and time** — quantitative is often faster to run at scale but needs careful instrument
  design upfront; qualitative is slower per respondent but needs fewer of them; pragmatic costs the
  most overall since it runs two research processes.
- **Suitability** — conclude that there's no universally "best" approach: the right choice depends
  entirely on whether the research question needs breadth (go quantitative), depth (go qualitative),
  or genuinely needs both to be properly answered (go pragmatic/mixed-methods).
