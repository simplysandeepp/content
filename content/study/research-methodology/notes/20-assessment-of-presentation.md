## The idea, in plain words

A written proposal, report, or thesis is one thing — but it also usually has to be **presented
out loud**, for example in a viva or a defense, and that oral presentation gets assessed separately
from how the written document itself is graded. A researcher can write an excellent report and still
present it poorly, or vice versa; this topic is specifically about what makes the *presentation*
of the work good, not the document.

## Typical assessment criteria for a presentation

- **Clarity of delivery** — can the audience actually follow what's being said, in real time, without
  the speaker's own notes to lean on? This mirrors the "Clear" principle from technical writing
  (topic 16), just applied to spoken delivery instead of a written page.
- **Depth of subject knowledge shown, especially when answering questions** — a presenter who only
  knows their prepared slides, and can't engage with a question that goes slightly off-script,
  demonstrates shallower understanding than one who can.
- **Quality and relevance of visual aids** — slides, diagrams, and charts should support and clarify
  what's being said, not distract from it or repeat it word-for-word; this is where the
  technical-writing principle of using visuals only where they genuinely communicate better than
  prose (topic 16) directly carries over into presentation design.
- **Logical structure / organisation of the talk** — the presentation should move through a clear,
  sensible order rather than jumping around, so the audience always knows where they are in the
  argument.
- **Time management** — staying within the allotted time while still covering everything important;
  running out of time partway through the findings, or finishing with ten minutes of silence left,
  both count against the presenter.
- **Ability to justify and defend methodology choices under questioning** — a panel will usually
  probe *why* a particular method, sample, or analysis was chosen; being able to defend those choices
  convincingly is often weighted as heavily as the original presentation itself.
- **Audience / panel engagement** — maintaining the audience's attention and responding naturally to
  their reactions and questions, rather than reading a script at them.
- **Composure in handling unexpected questions** — staying calm and thinking clearly under an
  unanticipated or difficult question, rather than freezing or becoming defensive.

## Tying the presentation back to the written work

An effective presentation doesn't invent a separate narrative from the underlying proposal or
report — it **mirrors the same structure**: context → problem → methodology → findings (or expected
outcomes, for a proposal) → conclusion. The presentation is essentially the same argument the written
document makes, delivered live and compressed to fit the time available, so a presenter who knows
their own written document well should already know the shape their talk needs to take.
