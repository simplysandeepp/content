## The idea, in plain words

Not every question is worth researching. A **good research problem** is one that's actually
possible to investigate, worth investigating, and will produce a result someone can trust and use.
Before committing months to a study, a researcher checks a problem against a short list of
characteristics — if it fails most of them, it gets reshaped or dropped.

## The characteristics

- **Novelty / originality** — it adds something new: a gap in existing knowledge, a fresh angle on
  an old question, or a new context (new population, new technology, new time period) for a known
  question. A problem that just repeats a settled answer isn't worth a study.
- **Clarity and specificity** — the problem is stated precisely enough that two different
  researchers would understand it the same way. "Study social media" is not a research problem;
  "Does daily Instagram use above 2 hours correlate with self-reported anxiety in college students?"
  is.
- **Feasibility** — it can actually be completed with the time, budget, data access, equipment, and
  skills available. A brilliant question that needs five years and a lab nobody has access to isn't
  feasible for most researchers.
- **Measurability / researchability** — the variables involved can be observed, measured, or tested
  empirically. Pure value judgements ("is modern art better than classical art?") aren't
  researchable in the scientific sense; they can't be settled with data.
- **Relevance / significance** — the answer matters to the field, to practice, or to society. It
  should fill a genuine gap, not just satisfy curiosity with no wider use.
- **Ethical soundness** — the study can be conducted without harming participants, violating
  privacy, or requiring data that can't be ethically obtained.

## Why these characteristics matter (the "why" the exam wants)

- **Novelty and relevance** protect against wasted effort — research that only repeats what's
  already known doesn't advance the field and is hard to get published or funded.
- **Clarity** keeps the whole study focused: a vague problem leads to a vague methodology, which
  leads to results nobody can act on.
- **Feasibility** is a practical filter — the best research problem in the world is worthless if it
  can never be completed within the real constraints a researcher has.
- **Measurability** is what separates a *research* problem from a philosophical or opinion-based
  question — without measurable variables, there's no way to collect evidence or draw a defendable
  conclusion.
- **Ethical soundness** protects participants and the researcher's own credibility; an ethically
  compromised study can be rejected outright regardless of how good its other qualities are.

Put together: a problem with all of these is one a researcher can actually start, finish, defend,
and have taken seriously.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Real mid-term, Q1 (K1, 3 marks): "Describe the characteristics of a good research problem and explain why these characteristics are important."

**Answer plan:** list the six characteristics above (novelty, clarity, feasibility, measurability,
relevance, ethical soundness) in one or two words each with a one-line description, then pair each
with its "why it matters" line from the section above. At K1/3 marks, keep each point to a single
sentence — this is a recall-and-list question, not an essay; a clean bulleted list scores full marks
faster than paragraphs.

### Practice set, Unit 2 (research problem) Q2: "Examine the criteria for selecting a good research problem and relate how factors such as feasibility, originality, and researcher's competence influence the outcome of a study."

**Answer plan:** this is the same characteristic list, but at a higher K-level (Examine/relate = K3
territory), so go beyond naming them — for each of feasibility, originality, and researcher's
competence specifically, explain the *consequence* of getting it wrong: low feasibility → the study
stalls or is abandoned partway; low originality → wasted effort, rejection by reviewers/examiners;
a mismatch between the problem and the researcher's own competence (statistical skill, domain
knowledge, access to the field) → poor-quality execution even if the problem itself was good. Note
that "researcher's competence" is its own factor, distinct from the problem's own qualities — a
good problem can still fail in the hands of the wrong researcher.
