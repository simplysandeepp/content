## The idea, in plain words

A **research problem** exists when there's a gap between what is currently known and what needs
to be known — or when there's some difficulty or question that genuinely needs systematic
investigation to resolve. It's not just "a topic you find interesting"; it has to be a real,
structured problem that research can actually be designed around.

## The five components of a true research problem

For something to count as a proper research problem (not just a vague curiosity), it needs all
five of these:

1. **An individual or group with the problem** — someone who actually faces the difficulty or
   needs the answer.
2. **Objectives to be achieved** — a clear goal the research is trying to reach.
3. **Alternative means of reaching those objectives** — more than one possible way to get there
   (if there's only one obvious way, there's nothing to investigate).
4. **Genuine doubt about which alternative is best** — real uncertainty about which option is
   correct or most effective; without this doubt, there's no question left to research.
5. **An environment/context** — the setting or conditions the problem exists in, which shapes
   which alternatives are even realistic.

## Types of research

Research doesn't fall into one single list of categories — it's described along several
*independent* dimension-pairs, and a given study can be classified on several of these at once.

- **Basic / fundamental** vs **applied** — basic research builds theory or knowledge for its own
  sake (e.g. a physicist studying a subatomic particle with no immediate product in mind); applied
  research solves a specific practical problem (e.g. a company researching how to make a battery
  charge faster).
- **Descriptive** vs **analytical** — descriptive research portrays "what is" (e.g. a survey
  describing current smartphone usage habits among teenagers); analytical research uses existing
  facts to critically evaluate "why" (e.g. analysing existing sales data to explain why a product's
  sales dropped).
- **Quantitative** vs **qualitative** — quantitative research is numerical/statistical (e.g.
  measuring the percentage of customers who prefer feature A over B); qualitative research is
  non-numerical and focused on meaning or quality (e.g. interviewing customers about *why* they feel
  loyal to a brand).
- **Conceptual** vs **empirical** — conceptual research is abstract and theory-building, often done
  by thinkers reasoning from existing ideas (e.g. developing a new theoretical model of motivation);
  empirical research is grounded in observed data or direct experience (e.g. running an experiment
  to test whether that motivation model actually predicts behaviour).

## A different three-way split: theoretical, applied, and action research

Separately from the pairs above, research problems are sometimes classified into three types based
on *who* is doing the research and *why*:

- **Theoretical research** — builds general knowledge or theory with no immediate practical goal;
  typically done in academic settings to advance understanding itself.
- **Applied research** — aims to solve one specific, real-world problem for a person or
  organisation.
- **Action research** — carried out by practitioners themselves (teachers, managers, nurses) to
  improve their own immediate practice while they study it. It's usually cyclical: plan → act →
  observe → reflect, then repeat with adjustments.

Each fits a different situation: theoretical research suits foundational academic work where
building knowledge is the point; applied research suits a concrete organisational problem that
needs a specific fix; action research suits a practitioner who is embedded in the situation and
wants to improve their own practice directly, in real time, while generating research knowledge
from doing so.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Real mid-term, Q2 (K1, 3 marks): "Identify the different types of research and provide an example of each."

**Answer plan:** at K1/3 marks this wants a short, clean list, not an essay. List the four
dimension-pairs — basic/applied, descriptive/analytical, quantitative/qualitative,
conceptual/empirical — and give exactly one real-world example per type (the examples above work
directly), keeping each to a single line.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q5: "Classify the different types of research problems (theoretical, applied, and action research) and analyze the situations in which each type would be most appropriate."

**Answer plan:** this question specifically wants the three-way split, not the dimension-pairs
above — define theoretical, applied, and action research as given above, then analyse the fitting
situation for each: theoretical for foundational academic work with no immediate practical aim,
applied for solving a specific organisation's concrete problem, and action research when the
researcher is a practitioner embedded in the situation who wants to improve their own practice
directly while studying it (note the plan → act → observe → reflect cycle as a concrete detail that
shows deeper understanding).

### Real mid-term, Q7 (K4, 8 marks): "Identify a specific research problem and formulate a detailed research problem statement. Your statement should address all key elements required in a well-defined research problem statement."

**Answer plan:** this is a K4/8-mark synthesis question — pick one concrete, specific problem (don't
stay abstract) and write out a full problem statement that explicitly labels each required element
as you go, drawing directly on the five components above plus the characteristics from topic 4 and
the scope/objectives from topic 6:

1. **Background/context** — briefly set the scene (e.g. "Online learning platforms have grown
   rapidly, but engagement data shows high drop-off rates mid-course.")
2. **The problem/gap itself** — state the specific difficulty or unanswered question clearly (e.g.
   "It is unclear which specific course-design factors most strongly predict whether undergraduate
   students disengage from an online course before completion.")
3. **Significance** — why it's worth answering (e.g. helps institutions redesign courses to reduce
   dropout, improves student outcomes).
4. **Scope and boundaries** — what's included/excluded (e.g. limited to undergraduate students, one
   institution, one semester — see topic 6).
5. **Objectives / research questions** — specific, measurable goals (e.g. "To identify the three
   course-design factors most correlated with mid-course disengagement among undergraduates at
   [institution] in [semester].").
6. **The individual/group affected and the alternatives/doubt** — name who faces the problem
   (students, instructors) and note that there's genuine uncertainty about which design factor(s)
   matter most (satisfying components 1, 3, and 4 from the five-component list above).

Writing it this way — one example, every element explicitly labelled — is what distinguishes a full
K4/8-mark answer from a vague paragraph: the examiner is checking that you know *all* the pieces a
real problem statement needs, not just that you can describe a topic.
