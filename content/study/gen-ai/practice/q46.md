## What's actually being asked
Explain, conceptually and mechanistically, what a diffusion model does — in particular, the two named processes: forward diffusion and reverse denoising.

## Answer

### The basic idea
A diffusion model learns to generate data by first learning to systematically *destroy* it. Take a real sample `x_0`, and over many small steps, add a little Gaussian noise each time until nothing recognizable is left — pure noise `x_T`. That corruption process is fixed and requires no learning. The generative part is the **reverse**: train a neural network to undo one small noising step at a time. Once trained, start from pure random noise and repeatedly apply the learned "denoise a little" step `T` times, and what comes out the other end is a brand-new, realistic sample — nothing was ever literally "decoded," it was built up by progressively removing noise the network was trained to recognize at every scale. In prose, the chain looks like:
`noise (x_T) → U-Net predicts ε_θ → denoise step → repeat ×T → generated x_0`.

### Forward diffusion process (fixed, not learned)
Each step adds a small, controlled amount of Gaussian noise, defined by a schedule `β_t`:
`q(x_t|x_{t-1}) = N(x_t; √(1−β_t) x_{t-1}, β_t I)`.
This is a Markov chain — each `x_t` depends only on `x_{t-1}` — and it has no trainable parameters; `β_t` is a fixed schedule, typically small and increasing with `t`. Because Gaussian noise composes cleanly, there's a closed-form shortcut straight from `x_0` to any `x_t` without simulating every intermediate step:
`x_t = √(ᾱ_t) x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_t) ε`, `ε ~ N(0,I)`,
where `α_t = 1 − β_t` and `ᾱ_t = Π_{s=1}^{t} α_s` is the cumulative product up to `t`. `ᾱ_t` shrinks as `t` grows (it's a product of numbers less than 1), so the original signal's weight `√(ᾱ_t)` shrinks while the noise's weight `√(1−ᾱ_t)` grows — by `t = T`, `x_T` is essentially indistinguishable from pure `N(0,I)` noise, which is exactly what makes it trivial to start sampling from.

### Reverse denoising process (learned)
The true reverse step `q(x_{t-1}|x_t, x_0)` would require knowing `x_0`, which isn't available at generation time. So a neural network (typically a U-Net, conditioned on the timestep `t`) is trained to approximate it by predicting the **noise** that was added: `ε_θ(x_t, t)`. Training objective:
`𝔼[‖ε − ε_θ(x_t, t)‖²]`
— a simple regression: sample real data, pick a random `t`, add the corresponding known noise via the closed form above, and train the network to recover exactly that noise. At generation time, start from `x_T ~ N(0,I)`, and repeatedly use `ε_θ(x_t, t)` to estimate and subtract the noise component, stepping back toward `x_{t-1}`, down to `x_0`.

## Exam tip
A common mistake is saying the forward process is "learned too" — it isn't; it's a fixed Markov chain defined entirely by the noise schedule `β_t`. Only the reverse process has trainable parameters.
