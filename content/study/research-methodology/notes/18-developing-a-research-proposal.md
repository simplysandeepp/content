## The idea, in plain words

A **research proposal** is written *before* the actual research begins. Its job is to convince a
supervisor, an academic committee, or a funding body that the proposed study is worth doing, and
that the researcher has a credible, workable plan for actually carrying it out. Where a research
report (topic 17) documents work that's already finished, a proposal is a pitch and a plan for work
that hasn't started yet.

## Why developing a proposal matters

A proposal does three distinct jobs at once:

- **It secures approval or funding** — a supervisor, committee, or funding agency reads the proposal
  to decide whether to let the study proceed or to pay for it. Without a convincing proposal, the
  research often doesn't get the green light at all.
- **It forces the researcher to clarify their own thinking before committing time** — writing down
  the problem, objectives, and method in enough detail to convince someone else usually exposes gaps
  or vague spots in the researcher's own plan that would otherwise only surface halfway through the
  actual study, when they're much more expensive to fix.
- **It becomes the working roadmap for the research itself** — once approved, the proposal doesn't
  get thrown away; it's the document the researcher refers back to throughout the study to stay on
  track, and it's often what the final report is later checked against.

## Key elements of a research proposal

- **Problem statement** — a clear statement of exactly what research problem the study will address
  and why it needs addressing.
- **Objectives** — the specific, stated goals the research aims to achieve.
- **A brief literature review** — enough of a summary of existing relevant work to show the gap the
  proposed study is meant to fill, demonstrating the researcher already knows the field.
- **Proposed methodology** — the plan for how the research will actually be carried out: design,
  sample, data collection, and analysis approach.
- **Expected outcomes / significance** — what the study is likely to find or contribute, and why that
  matters to the field, to practice, or to society.
- **A timeline** — a schedule showing when each stage of the research will be carried out.
- **A budget**, where relevant — an estimate of the costs involved, for proposals going to a funding
  body.
- **References** — the sources already cited in the literature review and elsewhere in the proposal.

## How this topic connects to the rest of the unit

Developing a research proposal sits as the conceptual bridge between two neighbouring topics in this
unit. It comes right after **topic 17 (Writing a Research Report)**, which documents research that's
already been done — a proposal is effectively the mirror image, written for research that's about to
be done. And it leads directly into **topic 19 (Format of a Research Proposal)**, which lays out the
detailed section-by-section structure a proposal actually follows in practice. The skill of writing a
good proposal also draws directly on the same technical-writing principles covered in **topic 16**:
a proposal still has to be clear, concise, correct, and complete, written for the specific audience
(the committee or funder) who will decide whether to approve it, and organised logically so that
audience can quickly judge the problem, the plan, and its feasibility.
