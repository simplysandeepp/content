## What's actually being asked
How GANs work as a two-player game, a diagram of the Generator-vs-Discriminator setup, and a full explanation of the minimax objective function.

## Answer

### The working principle
A GAN has two networks trained against each other:
- **Generator G** takes random noise `z ~ p_z` (usually `N(0,I)` or uniform) and transforms it into a fake sample `G(z)`, trying to make it look like it came from the real data distribution `p_data`.
- **Discriminator D** takes a sample (real x, or fake `G(z)`) and outputs a scalar `D(·) ∈ (0,1)`, its estimate of the probability that the sample is real.

They're trained with opposing goals: D wants to correctly tell real from fake; G wants to fool D into saying "real" about its fakes. Neither network sees hand-labelled "fake vs real" training examples in the usual supervised sense — the *only* supervision is this adversarial back-and-forth, which is what makes it a **minimax game**: G minimizes what D maximizes.

The diagram above traces this: noise z flows through G to produce a fake sample; both the fake and a real sample are fed into D; D's classification output feeds a loss/gradient signal back to update both networks.

### The objective function
`min_G max_D V(D,G) = 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[log D(x)] + 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[log(1 − D(G(z)))]`

Reading it term by term:
- `𝔼_{x∼p_data}[log D(x)]` — D wants this high, i.e., `D(x)→1` for real x.
- `𝔼_{z∼p_z}[log(1−D(G(z)))]` — D also wants this high, i.e., `D(G(z))→0`, correctly flagging fakes.
- **D's move**: `max_D` the full `V(D,G)` — it maximizes `log D(x) + log(1−D(G(z)))`, pushing it toward confidently separating real from fake.
- **G's move**: `min_G` the same `V(D,G)` — since G can't affect the first term (it only depends on real data and D), this reduces to minimizing `log(1−D(G(z)))`, i.e., G wants `D(G(z))→1` (fooling D into calling fakes "real").

### Why it's a genuine game, not just two separate losses
The two networks are updated **alternately**: freeze G, take a gradient-ascent step on D's objective; then freeze D, take a gradient step on G's objective; repeat for many iterations. Each network's loss landscape keeps shifting because the other network is also changing — textbook minimax/Nash-equilibrium territory, not ordinary single-objective optimization. At the theoretical optimum, D can no longer tell real from fake at all: `D(x) = D(G(z)) = 0.5` everywhere, and G's output distribution matches `p_data` exactly.

### A practical twist — the non-saturating generator loss
Early in training, D rejects G's (still poor) fakes confidently, so `D(G(z)) ≈ 0`, and `log(1−D(G(z)))` sits in a region where its gradient w.r.t. G's parameters is nearly flat (saturates) — G barely learns. In practice, G is instead trained to **maximize** `log D(G(z))` (the non-saturating loss) — the same fooling goal, but with much stronger gradients exactly when G most needs them (when it's doing badly).

## Exam tip
State explicitly that G never sees real data directly — it only ever gets a gradient signal relayed through D. This single fact is a favourite "trick" short-answer question on its own.
