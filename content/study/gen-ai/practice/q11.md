## What's actually being asked
Explain each VAE component's role, derive the ELBO/loss function's two terms from first principles, and justify why both terms are necessary.

## Answer

### Roles of each component (see the diagram below for the flow)
- **Encoder:** takes input `x` and outputs the parameters `μ(x)`, `σ(x)` of an approximate posterior `q(z|x) = N(μ, σ²)` over the latent space — it doesn't output a single point, it outputs a *distribution*.
- **Latent space:** the compressed probabilistic representation `z`; because the KL term during training pulls every input's `q(z|x)` toward the shared prior `p(z) = N(0,I)`, the space ends up continuous and structured, so nearby points decode to similar, meaningful outputs.
- **Reparameterization trick:** sampling `z ~ q(z|x)` is normally a non-differentiable operation (you can't backpropagate through a random draw). The trick rewrites the sample as `z = μ + σ ⊙ ε`, with `ε ~ N(0, I)` drawn independently — now the randomness is isolated in `ε`, and `z` is a differentiable function of `μ` and `σ`, so gradients can flow back through the encoder during training.
- **Decoder:** takes the sampled `z` and outputs (the parameters of) `p(x|z)`, reconstructing a full data sample `x̂` from the compressed, noisy latent code.

### Deriving the two loss components from the ELBO

Start from the (generally intractable) log-likelihood of the data:
`log p(x) = log ∫ p(x,z) dz`

Multiply and divide inside the integral by the encoder's distribution `q(z|x)` — this doesn't change the value, just rewrites it as an expectation under `q`:
`log p(x) = log 𝔼_{q(z|x)}[ p(x,z) / q(z|x) ]`

Apply **Jensen's inequality** (log is concave, so `log 𝔼[·] ≥ 𝔼[log ·]`):
`log p(x) ≥ 𝔼_{q(z|x)}[ log( p(x,z) / q(z|x) ) ]` — this right-hand side is the **ELBO** (evidence lower bound).

Expand `p(x,z) = p(x|z)·p(z)` and split the log of a ratio into a difference:
`ELBO = 𝔼_{q(z|x)}[ log p(x|z) + log p(z) − log q(z|x) ]`
`     = 𝔼_{q(z|x)}[ log p(x|z) ] − 𝔼_{q(z|x)}[ log q(z|x) − log p(z) ]`

The second term is, by definition, the KL-divergence between `q(z|x)` and the prior `p(z)`:
`𝔼_{q(z|x)}[ log q(z|x) − log p(z) ] = D_KL(q(z|x) ‖ p(z))`

So:
`log p(x) ≥ 𝔼_{q(z|x)}[log p(x|z)] − D_KL(q(z|x) ‖ p(z))` — the canonical ELBO.

Training maximizes this lower bound, which is equivalent to minimizing its negative — the VAE loss:
`L(θ,φ;x) = −𝔼_{q_φ(z|x)}[log p_θ(x|z)] + D_KL(q_φ(z|x) ‖ p(z))`

This has exactly **two components**:
1. **Reconstruction loss** `−𝔼_{q(z|x)}[log p(x|z)]` — how well the decoder reconstructs `x` from the sampled `z` (in practice, often MSE for continuous data or cross-entropy for binary/categorical data, both of which fall out of specific choices of `p(x|z)`).
2. **KL-divergence loss** `D_KL(q(z|x) ‖ p(z))` — how far the encoder's posterior is from the standard normal prior.

### Why both are required
Reconstruction loss alone (as in a plain autoencoder) gives no pressure on the *shape* of the latent space — the encoder could map each input to an arbitrary, far-apart point with near-zero variance, and the model would still reconstruct perfectly, but sampling a random `z ~ N(0,I)` afterward would land in a region nothing was ever trained to decode — generation would fail. KL-divergence alone, with no reconstruction term, would just push every `q(z|x)` to collapse onto the prior regardless of `x` ("posterior collapse") — the decoder would learn to ignore `z` and output some generic average sample, since there's no penalty for ignoring the input. Only together do they balance: reconstruction keeps the latent code *informative* about `x`, and KL keeps the latent space *well-structured and sample-able* — which is exactly what makes the VAE usable as a generator, not just a compressor.

## Exam tip
If asked to "derive" the loss, examiners want the Jensen's-inequality step shown explicitly — stating the ELBO formula without deriving it from `log p(x)` is a common way to lose derivation marks even when the final formula quoted is correct.
