## What's actually being asked
Map each observed symptom to its likely mechanism, then compare the standard, least-squares, and Wasserstein GAN objectives on how well each addresses stability and image quality for this medical-imaging setting.

## Answer

### Diagnosing the symptoms
- **Mode collapse** — G covers only a narrow slice of real anatomical variability; the standard sigmoid-based minimax loss gives no explicit diversity incentive (same mechanism discussed for digit GANs).
- **Unstable loss curves** — alternating gradient updates on a non-convex minimax game with a saturating sigmoid D; under the original objective, loss values don't correlate well with actual sample quality, so the curves swing without telling you much useful.
- **Artifacts** — typical when a strided-conv/transposed-conv G is pushed by a D that locally overpowers it, repeatedly finding and reacting to small checkerboard/edge "tells," and when gradients vanish or explode under the saturating loss, leaving some regions under-corrected.
- **Insufficient fine detail** — a classic symptom of D saturating on big, obvious fakes and never being forced to push G on local, high-frequency texture — gradient dries up before G is pressured to refine detail.

### Standard GAN objective
`min_G max_D V(D,G) = 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[log D(x)] + 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[log(1 − D(G(z)))]`

D has a sigmoid output and behaves as a binary classifier. Mechanism problem: once D confidently separates real MRI scans from fakes (easy early on, since initial fakes are poor), `log(1 − D(G(z)))` sits in a flat region of its curve and G's gradient vanishes — directly producing the vanishing-gradient and poor-fine-detail symptoms. Because the loss doesn't move smoothly with actual quality, the observed curves look unstable.

### Least-Squares GAN (LSGAN)
D loss: `min_D (1/2) 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[(D(x) − 1)²] + (1/2) 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[(D(G(z)))²]`
G loss: `min_G (1/2) 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[(D(G(z)) − 1)²]`

D has no sigmoid at its output — it's scored as a regression target (1 for real, 0 for fake) with squared error. Mechanism: unlike cross-entropy, squared error keeps penalizing a sample even once it's on the "correct" side of D's decision boundary but still far from it — it keeps pulling fakes continuously toward the real-data manifold instead of stopping once "good enough to fool D." This directly helps **fine detail** (G keeps receiving gradient to close the remaining gap) and gives **smoother loss curves**, since the penalty is a continuous quadratic rather than a saturating log. It reduces mode collapse somewhat but less decisively than Wasserstein-based methods.

### Wasserstein GAN (WGAN / WGAN-GP)
Critic objective (real-valued score, no sigmoid), with the critic constrained to be 1-Lipschitz (weight clipping, or the softer gradient-penalty variant):
`max_D 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[D(x)] − 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[D(G(z))]`
G objective: `min_G − 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[D(G(z))]`
WGAN-GP adds a penalty `λ 𝔼[(‖∇_x̂ D(x̂)‖₂ − 1)²]` on points `x̂` interpolated between real and fake samples, to softly enforce the Lipschitz constraint instead of hard clipping.

Mechanism: the Wasserstein (earth-mover) distance stays meaningful and gives non-zero, informative gradients even when the real and fake distributions barely overlap — exactly the early-training regime for a hard domain like MRI scans. This is the strongest answer to **mode collapse** (a smoother distance landscape gives less incentive to hide in one mode) and to **unstable loss curves** (the critic's loss now actually tracks sample quality, something the standard GAN loss never reliably gave). The Lipschitz constraint also caps how sharply the critic can react to small pixel-level changes, reducing **artifacts** driven by an overly reactive D.

### Comparison summary

| Objective | Vanishing gradients | Mode collapse | Loss ↔ quality correlation | Fine detail |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard GAN | high risk (sigmoid saturates) | high risk | poor | weak |
| LSGAN | reduced (quadratic, non-saturating penalty) | somewhat reduced | better (smooth quadratic) | improved |
| WGAN(-GP) | low (Lipschitz critic avoids saturation) | lowest (smooth EM distance) | best | best |

### Recommendation for this scenario
For synthetic MRI generation, where both texture fidelity and distributional coverage carry clinical weight, WGAN-GP is the strongest default — it addresses stability, diversity, and gives a loss signal that's actually usable for monitoring training. LSGAN is a reasonable lighter-weight fallback when full Lipschitz enforcement is too costly to train.
