## What's actually being asked
State the mathematical objective each paradigm optimizes, and walk through one classification example showing both approaches side by side.

## Answer

### Mathematical foundation

A classification problem is, at its core, a question about the relationship between input features `x` and a label `y`. There are exactly two mathematically valid ways to get from `x` to a prediction of `y`:

- **Generative learning** models the **joint distribution** `P(x,y)`. In practice this is done by learning the class prior `P(y)` and the class-conditional likelihood `P(x|y)` separately from data, since `P(x,y) = P(x|y)·P(y)`. At prediction time, Bayes' theorem converts this into what you actually want:
  `P(y|x) = P(x|y)P(y) / P(x)`, where `P(x) = Σ_y P(x|y)P(y)`.
  The model never directly optimizes the quantity it's evaluated on (`P(y|x)`) — it gets there indirectly, through the joint.

- **Discriminative learning** models `P(y|x)` **directly**, skipping the joint entirely. There's no term for `P(x)` or `P(x|y)` anywhere in the model — it only ever learns the boundary/mapping that best predicts `y` from `x`, typically by directly minimizing a loss like cross-entropy over `(x,y)` training pairs.

### Worked classification example: email spam detection
Let `y ∈ {spam, not-spam}` and `x` = presence of the word "free" in the email.

**Generative approach (Naïve Bayes):**
1. Estimate the prior from training data, e.g. `P(spam) = 0.4`, `P(not-spam) = 0.6` (*illustrative numbers — use your actual training split's counts*).
2. Estimate the likelihood, e.g. `P("free" | spam) = 0.7`, `P("free" | not-spam) = 0.1`.
3. Apply Bayes' theorem to classify: `P(spam | "free") = P("free"|spam)P(spam) / [P("free"|spam)P(spam) + P("free"|not-spam)P(not-spam)]`
   `= (0.7×0.4) / (0.7×0.4 + 0.1×0.6) = 0.28 / 0.34 ≈ 0.82`.
   The model built the joint first, then derived this number.

**Discriminative approach (Logistic Regression):**
The model directly learns weights `w, b` and outputs `P(spam|x) = σ(w·x + b)` (sigmoid), trained by minimizing cross-entropy loss between predicted and true labels over the training set — it never estimates `P("free"|spam)` or `P(spam)` as separate quantities at all; it fits the conditional boundary end-to-end.

Both can reach a similar final number for `P(spam|x)`, but the generative route passes through an explicit model of how spam/non-spam emails *generate* the word "free," while the discriminative route jumps straight to the decision — which is also why only the generative model could, in reverse, be asked to *generate* a plausible new spam-like email.

### Why this matters mathematically
The generative route's intermediate quantities (`P(spam)`, `P("free"|spam)`) are each individually meaningful and reusable — you could use the same `P("free"|spam)` estimate to answer a completely different question, like "how unusual is this email overall?" The discriminative route's learned weights `w, b` have no such standalone interpretation — they exist only to produce a good `P(y|x)`, nothing else. That's the real mathematical cost of skipping the joint: you gain a simpler, often more accurate training problem, but you lose every other probabilistic question the joint distribution could have answered.

## Exam tip
The phrase examiners are listening for is "the generative model learns `P(x,y)` and derives `P(y|x)` via Bayes' theorem, while the discriminative model learns `P(y|x)` directly, with no model of `P(x)`." Say it in those exact terms, then back it with the worked example.
