## The idea, in plain words

**Technical writing** is writing whose job is to pass on technical or specialised information to a
particular audience, clearly enough that they can understand it or act on it — a user manual, a
research report, a proposal, a set of specifications. It's the opposite of creative or literary
writing: there's no room for ambiguity, mood, or multiple interpretations. If a reader can
misunderstand a technical document, the document has failed at its one job.

## What counts as technical writing

Technical writing covers a range of document types, not just one format:

- **Manuals** — step-by-step instructions for using or operating something.
- **Reports** — technical reports, research reports, progress reports.
- **Proposals** — documents written to get approval or funding for a piece of work.
- **Specifications** — precise descriptions of requirements, dimensions, or standards a product or
  system must meet.
- **White papers** — in-depth explanations of a problem and a proposed solution, often used to
  persuade a technical or business audience.
- **Professional emails and memos** — when they're conveying technical or project information inside
  an organisation, they follow the same clarity standards as the longer documents above.

## The 4 C's of technical writing

These four words are the shorthand the whole subject is built around:

- **Clear** — the meaning comes across in one reading, with no possible second interpretation. Short
  sentences, specific words, and a logical order all serve this.
- **Concise** — says what's needed and nothing more. Padding, repetition, and unnecessary qualifiers
  waste the reader's time and can bury the actual point.
- **Correct** — every fact, figure, unit, and technical term is accurate. In technical writing, a
  small factual error (a wrong number, a mislabelled diagram) can cause a real-world mistake, not
  just an embarrassment.
- **Complete** — includes everything the reader needs to understand or act on the content, with
  nothing important left out or assumed. A manual missing one step is a manual that fails the
  reader at exactly the point they needed it most.

## Basic principles of good technical writing

- **Know your audience** — write to their actual level of background knowledge; don't over-explain
  to experts or under-explain to beginners.
- **Use simple, precise language** — plain words over fancy ones, and the exact right word over a
  vague approximation.
- **Avoid unnecessary jargon** — use technical terms only where the audience needs them and
  understands them; define any that might not be familiar.
- **Organise logically** — structure the document with clear headings and sections so a reader can
  follow the flow, or jump straight to the part they need.
- **Use visuals where they help** — a diagram, table, or chart can communicate a relationship or a
  set of numbers far faster than a paragraph of prose; use one whenever it genuinely does the job
  better than text would.
- **Revise and proofread carefully** — technical writing is rarely right on the first draft; careful
  revision is what actually produces the clarity and correctness the 4 C's demand.

## Qualities needed in the writer

Good technical writing depends on the person writing it having:

- **Strong subject knowledge** — you can't explain clearly what you don't understand well yourself.
- **Clarity of thought** — clear writing starts with clear thinking; a muddled idea produces a
  muddled sentence no matter how carefully it's edited.
- **Objectivity** — reporting what is actually true/observed, not what the writer wants to be true.
- **Attention to detail** — technical content is full of small facts (numbers, units, steps) where a
  single slip changes the meaning.
- **Ability to structure information for someone else** — the writer has to set aside their own
  familiarity with the subject and organise the content the way a reader who doesn't share that
  context would need it, not the way it happens to live in the writer's own head.

## Purpose of technical writing

The purpose of technical writing is to **inform, instruct, or persuade a specific audience
precisely and without ambiguity** — very often so that the reader can go on to take a specific
action correctly. For example, a user manual's whole purpose is to let someone operate a device
correctly without needing to call anyone or ask anyone else for help; a technical proposal's purpose
is to let a reviewer judge and approve a plan of work without needing to ask the writer to explain
it further.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 1 (technical writing) Q1: "Define technical writing and list the different types of documents it includes."

**Answer plan:** give the definition above (writing that conveys technical/specialised information
clearly to a specific audience for a practical purpose, as opposed to creative/literary writing),
then list the document types: manuals, reports, proposals, specifications, white papers, and
professional emails/memos in a technical context.

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q2: "State the four characteristics (4 C's) of technical writing."

**Answer plan:** name all four — Clear, Concise, Correct, Complete — with one line each explaining
what it means in practice, using the descriptions above (clear = one reading, no ambiguity; concise
= nothing unnecessary; correct = every fact/figure accurate; complete = nothing important missing).

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q3: "List the basic principles of good technical writing."

**Answer plan:** list the six principles above — know your audience, use simple/precise language,
avoid unnecessary jargon, organise logically with headings, use visuals where they help, revise and
proofread carefully.

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q4: "Name the qualities required for effective technical writing."

**Answer plan:** list the writer qualities above — strong subject knowledge, clarity of thought,
objectivity, attention to detail, and the ability to structure information logically for a reader
who doesn't share the writer's own context.

### Practice set, Unit 1 Q5: "State the purpose of technical writing."

**Answer plan:** state the purpose line above — to inform, instruct, or persuade a specific audience
precisely and without ambiguity, usually so they can take a specific action — then give one example
to make it concrete, such as a user manual's purpose being to let someone operate a device correctly
without needing to ask anyone else.
