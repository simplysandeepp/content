## What's actually being asked
One use case + example per listed domain, then two major challenges that cut across generative AI's adoption in general.

## Answer
Across every domain below, the underlying mechanism is the same (a model that learned a data distribution is sampled to produce new, task-relevant content) — only the modality and the stakes of a mistake change.

### Use cases by domain

- **Healthcare** — synthetic medical data generation and drug discovery. *Example:* a diffusion/GAN model generates synthetic MRI scans to augment a small dataset for training a tumor-detection classifier, or a VAE proposes new candidate drug molecules.
- **Education** — personalized tutoring and content generation. *Example:* an LLM-based tutor generates practice questions and step-by-step explanations tailored to a student's weak topics, adapting difficulty on the fly.
- **Entertainment** — procedural content and media generation. *Example:* GAN/diffusion-based tools generate game textures, character art, or voice/music tracks (e.g., AI-assisted game asset design, deepfake-style VFX for film).
- **Marketing** — automated creative and copy generation. *Example:* generative models produce multiple ad copy variants and product images for A/B testing campaigns at a scale no human copywriting team could match manually.
- **Software development** — code generation and completion. *Example:* an autoregressive transformer-based coding assistant suggests function implementations, writes boilerplate, and helps debug from natural-language prompts.
- **Content creation** — text, image, and video generation for publishing. *Example:* text-to-image diffusion models generate illustrations for blog posts/articles, and LLMs draft first-pass article copy that a human editor refines.

### Two major challenges associated with adoption

1. **Reliability and factual accuracy (hallucination).** Generative models — especially LLMs and image generators — can produce outputs that are fluent/realistic but factually wrong or anatomically/physically implausible. In healthcare and software development this is a serious risk (a hallucinated drug interaction fact, or confidently-wrong generated code); it forces a human-in-the-loop review step that slows down the very automation the adoption was meant to deliver.

2. **Data privacy, bias, and IP/copyright.** Training data can leak sensitive patterns (patient data memorization risk in healthcare), encode and amplify biases present in training data (e.g., marketing content skewing toward majority demographics), and raise unresolved copyright questions when generated content closely resembles copyrighted training examples (entertainment and content creation are most exposed here). Together these create legal and ethical adoption barriers on top of the purely technical ones.

A third, related challenge worth a line if the question allows more depth: **compute cost and infrastructure**. Training and serving large generative models (diffusion, LLMs) is expensive, which can price smaller organizations in education or marketing out of building their own models and push them toward third-party APIs — adding dependency and cost-predictability concerns on top of the technical ones.

## Exam tip
Keep each domain's example *concrete and one-line* — examiners skim for whether you actually named a realistic use case per domain rather than a generic restatement ("AI helps healthcare"). For the challenges, pick ones that genuinely span multiple domains listed (reliability and privacy/bias both do) rather than one narrow to a single industry.
