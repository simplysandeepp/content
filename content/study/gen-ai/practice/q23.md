## What's actually being asked
A detailed walkthrough of the vanilla (original, fully-connected/conv) GAN's two sub-networks, how information flows through them, and the associated objective function.

## Answer

### Generator network
Takes a latent noise vector `z ~ p_z` (e.g., 100-D, sampled from `N(0,I)` or `U(−1,1)`) as its only input. In the original (Goodfellow et al.) formulation this is a multi-layer perceptron with ReLU hidden layers and a sigmoid/tanh output; in the common image-generation variants it's a stack of transposed-convolution ("deconv") layers that progressively upsample a small spatial map into a full-resolution image, with batch normalization and ReLU between layers and a tanh at the output to match a [-1,1] pixel range. Output: `x_fake = G(z)`, same shape as a real data sample.

### Discriminator network
Takes a data-shaped sample — either a real x or a fake `G(z)` — and squeezes it down to a single scalar. Vanilla version: MLP with LeakyReLU hidden layers; image version: stack of (strided) convolution layers with LeakyReLU, downsampling spatially, flattened and passed through a final fully-connected layer with a sigmoid activation, giving `D(·) ∈ (0,1)` — interpreted as "the probability this input is a real sample."

### Information flow
`z → G → x_fake`, then `{x_fake, x_real} → D → D(x_fake), D(x_real)`. Information flows in one direction on the forward pass (noise → generated sample → classification), and the resulting scalar losses flow back along the same path in reverse on the backward pass — D's gradient updates its own weights directly, and G's gradient has to pass *through* D's weights (without updating them) to reach G's own parameters. The diagram above shows exactly this: noise feeds the generator, its output and a genuine real image both feed the discriminator, and the discriminator collapses either input down to a single real/fake probability.

Critically, G and D never exchange information except through this scalar probability and its gradient — G has no direct access to real data at all, and D has no access to z.

### Objective function
`min_G max_D V(D,G) = 𝔼_{x∼p_data}[log D(x)] + 𝔼_{z∼p_z}[log(1 − D(G(z)))]`

- D is trained to **maximize** `V(D,G)`: push `D(x)→1` on real samples and `D(G(z))→0` on fakes — standard binary cross-entropy classification, just applied to two different sample sources.
- G is trained to **minimize** the same `V(D,G)` — since the real-data term doesn't involve G at all, this is equivalent to minimizing `𝔼_{z}[log(1−D(G(z)))]`. In practice, the **non-saturating** variant is used instead: G maximizes `𝔼_z[log D(G(z))]`, which gives much stronger gradients when D is confidently rejecting G's (still-poor) early samples, avoiding the vanishing-gradient problem of the original formulation.
- At the (theoretical) global optimum of this minimax game, `p_G = p_data` and `D(x) = 0.5` everywhere — D genuinely can no longer tell real from fake.

### Why "vanilla"
This architecture is called vanilla precisely because it has none of the later add-ons — no class conditioning (cGAN), no progressive growing or style-based latent injection (StyleGAN), no architectural tricks for training stability beyond the basic conv/FC stack described above. It's the baseline every GAN variant is compared against.

## Exam tip
When asked for "architecture in detail," explicitly separate the two networks' layer types and activation functions (don't just say "generator makes fakes, discriminator checks them") — the layer-level detail (batchnorm placement, LeakyReLU in D, tanh output in G) is what separates a full-marks answer from a one-paragraph summary.
