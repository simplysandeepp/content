## The idea, in plain words

**Research ethics** is the set of moral principles that govern how a researcher designs, conducts,
and reports a study. It isn't a side concern layered on top of "real" research — it's what makes
research results trustworthy in the first place. A study with brilliant methodology but a broken
ethical foundation (fabricated data, harmed participants, an undisclosed conflict of interest) can
be thrown out entirely, no matter how sound its statistics look.

## The core principles

- **Honesty** — accurate reporting of methods, data, and results; never fabricating (inventing data
  that was never collected) or falsifying (altering real data to get a desired result).
- **Objectivity** — minimising personal bias in how the study is designed, how the data is analysed,
  and how results are interpreted.
- **Integrity** — consistency between the principles a researcher *claims* to follow and what they
  actually *do* in practice.
- **Confidentiality** — protecting participants' identity and personal data, so taking part in a
  study doesn't expose someone to unwanted risk or exposure.
- **Informed consent** — participants genuinely understand what the study involves and voluntarily
  agree to take part, before it starts, without being pressured or misled.
- **Non-maleficence** — avoiding causing harm (physical, psychological, social, or financial) to
  participants.
- **Respect for intellectual property** — giving proper credit for others' words, ideas, and data.
  This principle directly overlaps with "14. Plagiarism" — plagiarism is, at its core, a violation of
  this specific ethical principle.
- **Accountability** — the researcher takes responsibility for the work and its consequences, rather
  than deflecting blame if something goes wrong.

## Why research ethics matters

Ethical conduct protects the people taking part in a study from real harm. Beyond that, it's the
precondition for the wider scientific and public community being able to trust published findings at
all — a reader has no way to independently verify that a researcher collected and reported data
honestly, so the whole system of published research runs on an assumption of ethical conduct. When
that assumption is violated — through fabricated data, undisclosed harm to participants, or a hidden
conflict of interest — it doesn't just damage that one study; it can invalidate otherwise competent
research entirely, because the reader can no longer trust *any* part of it.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 2 (research problem/ethics) Q8: "Design a set of research ethics guidelines and justify the importance of each principle included."

**Answer plan:** turn the principle list above into a short numbered guideline document, with one
justification line per principle — e.g. (1) Honesty: report data exactly as collected, because
fabricated results are worthless and dangerous if acted on; (2) Objectivity: minimise bias at every
stage, because a biased design or analysis can produce a conclusion that was never really supported
by the evidence; (3) Confidentiality: protect participant data, because without this guarantee
people won't participate honestly, if at all; (4) Informed consent: get genuine, voluntary agreement
before starting, because participants have a right to know what they're agreeing to; (5)
Non-maleficence: avoid harming participants, because the research exists to produce knowledge, not
to cause damage along the way; (6) Respect for intellectual property: credit others' work properly,
because taking credit for someone else's contribution is a form of theft; (7) Accountability: the
researcher owns the outcomes of their own work, good or bad.

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q11: "Evaluate how research ethics influences the credibility and trustworthiness of research findings."

**Answer plan:** state plainly that ethics is a *precondition* for credibility, not a separate
add-on bolted onto good methodology. Without demonstrable ethical conduct — genuine consent, honest
reporting, no fabrication, no undisclosed conflicts of interest — published results can't be trusted
even if the methodology looks technically sound on paper, because the reader has no way to verify
that the data itself is real and uncorrupted. Evaluate with the flip side too: a single exposed
ethics violation (a retraction over fabricated data is the classic case) tends to cast doubt backward
over a researcher's *entire* body of work, not just the one flawed study — which is exactly why the
research community treats ethical violations as more serious than most methodological weaknesses.

### Real mid-term, Q9 (K5, 10 marks) — OR option: "Design a framework that integrates research ethics principles, and real-life case studies for new researchers."

This is a K5/10-mark synthesis question — the single biggest-weight answer in the whole exam — so it
needs a genuinely structured, multi-part framework, not one paragraph gesturing at "ethics matters."

**Answer plan — a four-part training framework:**

1. **Foundation: the core principles.** Lay out the eight principles from the section above (honesty,
   objectivity, integrity, confidentiality, informed consent, non-maleficence, respect for
   intellectual property, accountability) as the framework's foundation layer — every other part of
   the framework refers back to these.

2. **Learning layer: one real-life-style case per principle.** Pair each principle with a realistic
   case a new researcher can learn from, so the principle isn't just an abstract rule:
   - *Honesty* — a researcher fabricates or touches up data to get a cleaner result; the paper is
     later retracted when another team can't reproduce the finding.
   - *Objectivity* — a study funded by a company is designed and analysed in a way that quietly
     favours that company's product, without disclosing the conflict of interest.
   - *Confidentiality* — participant data from a health study is stored insecurely and leaks,
     exposing people's private medical information.
   - *Informed consent* — participants in a psychological study are misled about what's actually
     being tested, and only told the truth (if at all) after the fact, without a chance to have
     genuinely agreed upfront.
   - *Non-maleficence* — an experiment causes participants real psychological distress that wasn't
     disclosed or planned for beforehand.
   - *Respect for intellectual property* — a researcher copies large sections of another paper's
     literature review without citation (the direct overlap with "14. Plagiarism").
   - *Integrity* — a researcher publicly claims a rigorous peer-reviewed process while privately
     skipping proper review steps to publish faster.
   - *Accountability* — when a study's result turns out to be wrong, the researcher blames a
     co-author or lab assistant instead of taking responsibility for the published work.

3. **Decision checklist.** A short, practical checklist a new researcher runs through *before*
   starting any study: Have participants given genuine informed consent? Is there a plan to protect
   their confidentiality? Could this study cause harm, and has that been minimised? Is there any
   conflict of interest that needs disclosing? Is every source and prior contribution properly
   credited? Is the data being reported exactly as collected, with no pressure to "improve" it?

4. **Escalation and reporting path.** What a new researcher should actually do if an ethical concern
   comes up mid-study: pause and document the concern; raise it with a supervisor or the
   institution's research ethics committee/institutional review board rather than deciding alone;
   follow the institution's formal reporting procedure if the concern involves misconduct by someone
   else; and know that raising a genuine concern early is always treated far better, by any
   reasonable institution, than letting a violation surface later during peer review or after
   publication.

Presented together — principles, cases, checklist, escalation path — this is a complete, usable
framework a new researcher could actually follow, which is what a K5/10-mark "design a framework"
question is asking for.
