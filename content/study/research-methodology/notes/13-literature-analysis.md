## The idea, in plain words

Doing a literature *review* means gathering the relevant sources. **Literature analysis** is what
happens next: actually working through those sources critically and in a structured way, rather than
just listing what each paper found one after another. A good literature analysis reads like an
argument built from the existing evidence, ending in a clear statement of what's still missing.

## The typical structure, stage by stage

1. **Introduction** — states the scope and purpose of the review: what question it's covering and
   why, so the reader knows what to expect.
2. **Body, organised thematically or chronologically** — groups the existing studies around themes
   or tracks how understanding of the topic developed over time, rather than just listing sources in
   whatever order they were found.
3. **Critical appraisal of each source** — judging methodology quality, sample size, potential bias,
   and relevance to the question at hand, not just summarising what each source concluded.
4. **Synthesis across sources** — pulling the individual appraisals together to identify patterns,
   contradictions between studies, and — most importantly — the specific gap the new research can
   address.
5. **Citation tracking** — following references **backward** (who did this paper cite — the
   foundational work the field is built on) and **forward** (who has since cited this paper — the
   newest work building on it). This catches both the classics and the most current developments
   that a simple keyword search might miss entirely.
6. **Conclusion** — explicitly links everything reviewed back to the new research problem: here's
   what's known, here's the gap, here's where this new study fits.

## Why critical appraisal matters

Critical appraisal is the quality filter on the whole review. Without it, a literature review is
just a list of "paper said X" statements with no judgement about whether any of them are actually
trustworthy. With it, only credible, methodologically sound sources end up shaping the direction of
the new study — a weak or biased prior study doesn't get to quietly set the foundation for new work.

## Why citation tracking matters

A keyword search alone can miss a lot — papers that use different terminology, or very recent papers
that haven't accumulated the "right" keywords in their abstract yet. Citation tracking surfaces both
the most *influential* prior work (the papers everyone else cites, found by tracking backward) and
the most *current* related research (found by tracking forward from a known key paper), filling in
exactly the gaps a plain keyword search leaves behind.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 3 (literature/data) Q3: "Assess the typical structure of a literary analysis and justify the importance of each stage."

**Answer plan:** walk through the six stages above in order, giving one line justifying each:
introduction orients the reader; thematic/chronological organisation keeps the body from being a
disconnected list; critical appraisal filters for quality; synthesis is where the actual insight
comes from, not just the summary; citation tracking fills gaps a keyword search leaves; the
conclusion is what actually earns the review its place in the new study by tying it to the new
problem.

### Practice set, Unit 3 Q4: "Evaluate the role of critical appraisal and citation tracking in an effective literature review."

**Answer plan:** as explained above — critical appraisal exists to raise the *quality* of the review
(filtering out weak or biased sources before they can shape the new study), while citation tracking
exists to raise the *completeness* of the review (catching influential and current work that a
keyword search alone misses). Evaluate that a review missing either one is incomplete in a different
way: missing appraisal risks building on weak foundations; missing citation tracking risks missing
either the field's foundational work or its newest developments.

### Practice set, Unit 3 Q6: "Critically examine how literature analysis contributes to formulating a strong research problem."

**Answer plan:** the synthesis stage of literature analysis is specifically where gaps, contradictions,
and unanswered questions in the existing body of work get surfaced — and those gaps are themselves
usually the direct source of a well-grounded, original research problem. This ties literature
analysis directly back to "3. Sources of Research Problem," where "review of existing literature" is
listed as one of the most productive sources of a research problem: literature analysis is the
concrete process that turns that general source into an actual, specific, defensible problem
statement.
