## What's actually being asked
A step-by-step account of the GAN training loop (what happens each iteration) and specifically how G's output quality improves over many rounds of feedback from D.

## Answer

### One training iteration, step by step
1. **Sample a minibatch** of real data `x ~ p_data` and a minibatch of noise `z ~ p_z`.
2. **Generate fakes**: `x_fake = G(z)`.
3. **Update D**: with G's weights frozen, compute `V(D,G) = 𝔼[log D(x)] + 𝔼[log(1−D(G(z)))]` on this minibatch, and take a gradient-**ascent** step on D's parameters — D gets slightly better at telling this round's real from this round's fake.
4. **Update G**: with D's weights now frozen at their just-updated values, sample fresh noise, generate fresh fakes, and take a gradient step on G's parameters using the non-saturating objective `max_G 𝔼[log D(G(z))]` (or the original `min_G 𝔼[log(1−D(G(z)))]`) — G adjusts its weights in the direction that would have made D's current judgement of its fakes more favourable.
5. **Repeat** for many thousands of iterations, re-sampling real and noise batches each time.

### Why alternating, not joint
D and G are never updated from a shared objective in one simultaneous pass — each update step treats the other network as a temporarily fixed function. This matters because the "game" only makes sense if each player optimizes against the *current* strategy of its opponent, not a stale or simultaneously-moving target. In practice, frameworks implement this by simply ordering the two optimizer-step calls and detaching gradients appropriately between them.

### How G gets better over time — the feedback mechanism
Early in training, G's weights are random, so `G(z)` is noise-like garbage; D, seeing an easy contrast between this and real data, quickly learns to assign `D(G(z))≈0` almost everywhere. That gradient, backpropagated through D's frozen weights into G, tells G exactly which direction in its parameter space would have nudged this batch's fakes to look slightly more "real" according to D's current decision boundary. G takes a small step in that direction. Because this repeats every iteration, with D continually re-sharpening its boundary against G's *latest* fakes, G is chasing a target that keeps moving to stay just ahead of it — each step, G closes the gap on D's present weaknesses, and D, in turn, has to find new weaknesses to flag. Over many rounds this co-evolution is what pushes `G(z)`'s distribution to gradually match `p_data` more and more closely, rather than G ever being told directly "this pixel should be darker" the way a supervised loss would.

### Signs of healthy vs unhealthy training
- **Healthy**: D's accuracy on real-vs-fake hovers moderately (not pinned at 100%, not stuck at chance) — both networks are still learning from each other.
- **Unhealthy**: D becomes too strong too fast (saturates to near-perfect classification) — `D(G(z))→0` everywhere, G's gradient vanishes (the saturation problem addressed by the non-saturating loss), and G stops improving. This is why the non-saturating generator loss and a careful learning-rate/architecture balance between G and D matter in practice.
- **Mode collapse**: G may find it easier to fool D by producing only a few highly convincing sample types rather than the full diversity of `p_data` — a known failure mode of this alternating training process.
