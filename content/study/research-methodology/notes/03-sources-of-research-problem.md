## The idea, in plain words

A research problem doesn't just appear out of nowhere — it comes from somewhere real. Knowing the
common **sources** of research problems is useful both for finding your own problem and for
explaining, in an exam, where good problems typically come from.

## Where research problems actually come from

- **Personal or professional experience** — real difficulties a researcher encounters firsthand in
  their own field or job, which they then turn into a formal question to investigate.
- **Review of existing literature** — reading what's already been published often reveals gaps,
  contradictions, or questions nobody has fully answered yet.
- **Intuition and creative thinking** — a researcher's own "why does this happen?" curiosity,
  sometimes before any literature or data points to it.
- **Discussions with experts, mentors, and peers** — conversations that surface problems a
  researcher wouldn't have noticed alone, especially from people closer to the practical side of a
  field.
- **Current social, economic, or technological issues** — real-world developments (a new
  technology, an economic shift, a social trend) that raise new questions needing study.
- **Replication of an existing study in a new context** — repeating a known study with a new
  population, a new place, or at a new point in time, to see if the original findings still hold.
- **Sponsoring-agency or funding-call requirements** — many research problems are shaped (or
  directly specified) by the organisation funding the study, which defines the problem area it wants
  investigated.

## Exam questions this topic answers

### Practice set, Unit 2 Q1: "Analyze the various sources of a research problem and justify how prior research and intellectual curiosity contribute to identifying a good research problem."

**Answer plan:** first list all the sources above so the "analyze the various sources" part is
fully covered. Then specifically justify the two sources the question names:

- **Prior research (review of literature)** — this is the single most productive and most common
  professional source, because a careful literature review doesn't just confirm what's known; it
  actively surfaces gaps, contradictions between studies, and questions earlier researchers raised
  but never answered. A researcher reading broadly in a field is almost guaranteed to find at least
  one legitimate unanswered question this way.
- **Intellectual curiosity** — a researcher's own genuine "why does this happen?" question, arising
  from observation rather than reading, often leads to problems that are genuinely novel — the kind
  of question literature alone wouldn't surface, because nobody has thought to write about it yet.
  Curiosity is what drives a researcher to notice something odd in the first place, before any
  formal literature search even begins.

Conclude by noting that these two sources work well together in practice: curiosity generates the
initial spark of a question, and the literature review then confirms whether it's genuinely unanswered
and worth pursuing.
